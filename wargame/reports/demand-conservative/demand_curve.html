Firm orders: the first slice of your demand

The game holds 4,769 737 MAX and 7,330 A320neo-family firm orders (Cirium, October 2026). Your table has its own old-generation replacement (12.3k aircraft), and part of the firm orders serves growth, so counting the firm orders on top of your demand would count the same customers twice. In this game they are part of it.

  • Each year's demand is served first from the open firm orders, split between the makers in proportion to what each has left (about 39% Boeing, 61% Airbus). Only the rest is new customers, split by preference.
  • Deliveries follow your curve. The firm orders last until 2035; from then all demand is new customers (1,386 in 2035, then the whole curve).
  • Firm orders never switch maker or lapse. If a maker cannot build its share in a year, those orders wait and do not draw on later demand again.
  • The alternative not used (option A) kept the game's old approach: drop your old-generation column and deliver the firm orders on top of the rest, as fast as the lines allow. It moves your old-generation demand forward to about 2033 and double counts the growth already in the backlog.

Method

  • Fit. For each component, the 2026-2070 path with the least squared second differences (the smoothest), counted over the whole range, including where a component starts from or runs down to zero. It passes through your values in all eleven years, makes total demand match your four period totals exactly, keeps every value at zero or above, and keeps the fleet (19.7k at end-2026 plus each year's growth) within ±50 aircraft of your fleet column (its rounding; the largest gap is 50). Solved exactly (active-set quadratic programme) and cross-checked with a general-purpose solver.
  • Zeros from your table. Old-generation replacement is 0 from 2050 and post-2026 replacement is 0 to 2045, as in your table. Held at zero by the non-negativity limit: MAX/neo/A220 2027, post-2026 2046, post-2026 2047, post-2026 2048; post-2026 replacement first appears in 2049.
  • Rounding. Two of your totals are one above their parts (2045: 2,130 against 936 + 1,193; 2051: 1,957 against 934 + 1,022); the curve keeps the parts.
  • Class share. Your table covers the whole single-aisle market, A220 included. The game models the 737/A320 market and applies its class share (94.83%, from the Cirium fleet and order scope) once.
  • After 2070. The game holds the 2070 values (2,531 a year). That matters only for a programme entering service in 2052 or later, whose 20-year window runs past 2070.
  • Source. dashgame/make_demand_conservative.py writes demand_conservative.json and asserts every check above; run dash-2050-cons uses it through its sd_options.json.
