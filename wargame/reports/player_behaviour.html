Boeing

A gated, finance-led incumbent

How it was built

  • 151 calls, conferences and investor days, 2006-2025
  • 16 10-Ks; GS and MS models
  • 2,537 evidence items
  • Team: Ortberg (107 items), Malave (11, one call), Pope (8)

How it behaves

  1. Readiness gates first: no fps and no 787 Re-engine in round 1, and no rate step under the round-1 supply crunch
  2. Launch only if the business case closes: beat Do Nothing by >$1B, and lose ≤$2B to it in the slip test
  3. One major development at a time; never cancel; never blame
  4. Commit only to a committed engine; the engine requirement names no maker
  5. Widebody as Chicken: never follow an A350 Re-engine, move first when uncontested
  6. Calm, date-averse voice: “We're turning it. I don't think it's turned”

What it did

Game 1 · fps on timeWaited in round 1. Launched fps in 2031 (Solo, 10-year ramp-up) with the 737 rate step. Held incumbency at 40.2%.
Game 2 · fps three years lateThe same test rejected the late fps (−0.73 vs Do Nothing; −4.71 in the slip test). Launched a 787 Re-engine on GE in 2031 instead.

Airbus

Disciplined and rule-bound; maximises PV inside red lines

How it was built

  • Own voice: the FY2025 Board Report (310 items)
  • 183 items of Airbus behaviour seen by Boeing and analysts, 2006-2025
  • 867 evidence items
  • Faury: one own-words line; Toepfer 7 items; Wagner 2

How it behaves

  1. Own technology clock: NGSA in 2028, never re-timed around Boeing
  2. Asks for the partner's best engine but never waits for it
  3. No concurrent developments under the supply crunch
  4. Widebody Chicken: stays out once Boeing has re-engined the 787
  5. Delay Tactics at most once, only against an fps in development and only if worth ≥$1B (refused in game 1 at +0.72); poaches only while developing
  6. Signals to pull suppliers, then resets openly (“subject to Board approval”)

What it did

Game 1 · fps on timeNGSA 2028, then the A350 Re-engine in round 2 before Boeing could move. Refused Delay Tactics in round 3.
Game 2 · fps three years lateNGSA 2028. Signalled an A350 Re-engine for 2037, then shelved it after Boeing's 787 Re-engine.

Rolls-Royce

Cash-first and maturity-first, and structurally a round late

How it was built

  • 1,335 items from its own calls, 2010-2025
  • MS model, Capital IQ, 2019 engine brief
  • 1,951 evidence items
  • Team: Erginbilgic, McCabe, Watson (27 items, 26 from one event)

How it behaves

  1. “No airframe, no engine”: UltraFan only for an aircraft announced in an earlier round (unannounced = P 0.1)
  2. Time on wing first: the Trent 1000 upgrade in round 1
  3. Never compresses maturity; launches the widebody engine a year ahead
  4. Standard terms in every round; no overlapping UltraFan programmes
  5. Commit and honour, including the commitment to stop

What it did

Game 1 · fps on timeHeld the rule in round 1. Launched UltraFan narrowbody on Boeing's engine requirement in round 2; Boeing named GTF2. Cancelled it in round 3.
Game 2 · fps three years lateHeld the rule in round 1 and waited in round 2. Committed the UltraFan widebody in 2036 on Airbus's 2037 signal; Airbus shelved the A350 the same round.

Pratt & Whitney

Durability first, then cut what nobody selected

How it was built

  • 1,413 items from RTX/UTC calls; 413 from 10-Ks
  • GS RTX model and GTF reporting
  • 2,072 evidence items
  • Team: Calio (110), Mitchill (180), Eddy (21, one event)

How it behaves

  1. Fix forward: books the GTF durability upgrade first
  2. Hedged NGSA with a GTF2 for 2030, cleared by its objectives (profile default: a disclosed offer, 2029)
  3. CFO cuts an engine nobody selected at the first chance: “reaffirm, then cut”
  4. No discounts; no widebody engine
  5. Joint Venture only after an airframer names UltraFan

What it did

Game 1 · fps on timeUpgrade plus a 2030 GTF2 hedge. Cancelled GTF2 in round 2, the same sealed round Boeing named it.
Game 2 · fps three years lateThe same orders. Cancelled GTF2 in round 2, breaking its round-1 promise to keep it to the end of that round.

CFM/GE

The incumbent that wins by not moving

How it was built

  • 1,300 items from GE calls and investor days, 2015-2025
  • GS and MS CFM/Safran model cells
  • Culp: 474 own-words items
  • Safran side thin: the Safran gate is consent with no observed veto

How it behaves

  1. Derivative first: no new engine without a committed airframe
  2. Durability first, one commitment a turn: LEAP upgrade, then the GEnx package
  3. Tests its own defensive-engine trigger against PV and a $2B cap, and rejects it
  4. RISE open fan only with a committed airframe
  5. Guides low; never names a rival (“we don't have a birthright on that next order”)

What it did

Game 1 · fps on timeLEAP upgrade, then GEnx package, then nothing. NGSA, fps and the A350 Re-engine all fell back to its engines.
Game 2 · fps three years lateThe same. NGSA flew the LEAP derivative; Boeing chose the GE GEnx upgrade for the 787 Re-engine.

Market cell

Airlines and lessors

Priced engine certainty and consistent disclosures, keeping multipliers between 0.95 and 1.07. It priced the same round-1 NGSA facts at x0.97 in game 1 and x1.03 in game 2, citing the fps delay; that alone moved Boeing's Do Nothing value from −2.30 to −2.40. Its total effect on payoffs was at most about $0.5B, and it changed no decision.

How the behaviours combined

Widebody requests fall back to the GE GEnx upgrade, which needs no new programme and is always available. Because a request costs the airframer nothing, every contested aircraft in both games ended on CFM/GE engines.

1“No airframe, no engine” rules under sealed movesRolls-Royce waited for an aircraft announced in an earlier round; P&W cut an engine nobody had selected; CFM/GE launched nothing without a committed airframe. The airframers counted only committed engines but requested rivals' engines anyway, because a request falls back to CFM/GE at no cost. Four engine coordination failures followed, and every contested aircraft went to CFM/GE.
2Order of entry into service decides the narrowbodyThe first new aircraft in service gains share only until the second arrives, then shares freeze. NGSA entered in 2035; fps followed in 2038 in game 1 and never in game 2. Airbus held 59.8% in game 1 and reached 75% in 2050 in game 2.
3In widebody, whoever moves first winsBoeing's one-development rule decided who moved first. In game 1 fps was in development, so Airbus re-engined the A350 first. In game 2 there was no fps, so Boeing re-engined the 787 first and Airbus stayed out. The engine went to GE both times.
4Disclosures were read in opposite waysBoeing's engine-neutral requirement led Rolls-Royce to launch and Pratt & Whitney to cancel. Airbus's conditional 2037 A350 signal led Rolls-Royce to commit, in the round Airbus shelved it. P&W's “keep it until” promise was broken the next round.
5The fps delay worked entirely through behaviourRound 1 was identical in both games, and on the orders actually played in game 2 the delay had no direct effect. Applied to Boeing's game-1 plan, it would have cost Boeing $11.1B; the players' changed choices won back $8.9B of it.

Caveats

  • Thin seats decided some real moves: Boeing's CFO (one call; the game-2 slip-leg veto), P&W's operating head (one event; the 2030 hedge timing), and Rolls-Royce's CFO co-signed the game-1 bet. Airbus's thin seats only ratified the default; Boeing's Commercial Airplanes seat was played by doctrine; Rolls-Royce's Civil head rests on one event; CFM/GE's operating-head evidence ends March 2024.
  • Placeholder parameters sit on the decisive mechanics: the LEAP-derivative capture (0.92) and margin, fps capex, Joint Venture terms, capture speed, the 80% share cap and the 2060 horizon.
  • One game per scenario and one seed; round 1 was identical in both, so it is effectively one round-1 sample.
  • Counterfactuals hold the other players' recorded orders fixed, so they do not capture rivals' reactions.
  • PV is at each player's own WACC, so values cannot be added across players.
