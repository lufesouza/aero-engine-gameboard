Narrowbody and widebody war game, 2026-2045

Boeing, Airbus, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney and CFM/GE played three sealed rounds, ending in 2030, 2035 and 2045. Each was an independent agent, deciding as its own named executive team from profiles built from evidence. The game master alone held the full game-theory board, and passed on only what players chose to make public.

Players: Boeing, Airbus, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney, CFM/GE. Rounds: 2026–2030, 2031–2035, 2036–2045. Money: $B; delta PV is each player's full-game present value (to 2026, at its own WACC) versus the status quo in which nobody moves.

Summary

How the fight ended

Airbus won the narrowbody race in round 1. It launched NGSA in 2028, the best year for it in every engine case, and NGSA entered service in 2035, three years before Boeing's fps (launched 2031, in service 2038). Airbus ends at +$36.5B of delta PV and Boeing at +$3.9B. The narrowbody split moves from 40/60 to 42/58 in 2035 on Boeing's 737 rate step, and settles at 40.2/59.8 from 2040 once both new aircraft are in service.

The engine story is three coordination failures. Each time an airframer asked for a new engine, its maker had not committed it, or withdrew it in the same sealed round:

  • NGSA asked for UltraFan; Rolls-Royce was waiting for an airframe announcement.
  • fps asked for Pratt & Whitney's next-generation GTF, which Pratt & Whitney cancelled in that round.
  • The A350 Re-engine asked for the UltraFan widebody, which was never launched.

All three fell back to CFM/GE's existing engines: both new single-aisles fly the LEAP derivative and the A350 Re-engine flies the GEnx upgrade.

CFM/GE never launched a new engine. It ends with every narrowbody engine delivered from 2035 and 85% of widebody engines by 2045, worth +$18.8B. Rolls-Royce ends at -$7.0B: it launched an UltraFan narrowbody that no airframe chose and cancelled it in 2036, and it lost its sole-source A350 position to the GEnx upgrade from 2040. Pratt & Whitney ends at -$2.8B: it lost its A320neo share once NGSA entered service on the LEAP derivative, and cancelled its next-generation GTF in the round Boeing named it.

Four of the ten assigned objectives were met:

  • Boeing defended its incumbency;
  • Airbus protected the A320 while it carried the line;
  • Pratt & Whitney funded its durability upgrade;
  • CFM/GE dominated narrowbody engines.

Boeing's 50/50 split (40.2%) and Airbus's 60/40 edge (59.8%, short by 0.2pp) were missed, as were Rolls-Royce's narrowbody entry and widebody dominance, Pratt & Whitney's GTF base, and CFM/GE's open fan. Both new single-aisles were committed before 2036, so no airframe was left to fly RISE by 2045.

Key findings

  • Timing mattered more than technology. Airbus's 2028 NGSA locked in the narrowbody lead on an old-technology engine. Boeing's own rules ruled out a round-1 fps, a choice the engine prices at $3.4B of round-1 regret, and that put the 50/50 split out of reach.
  • 'No engine without an airframe' meets sealed, simultaneous moves. Rolls-Royce's round-1 regret ($20.2B) is the largest in the game: launching UltraFan with Airbus's NGSA would have won it. Engine makers and airframers each waited for the other to commit first.
  • Incumbency paid. CFM/GE's best move was to do nothing new in every round: the fallback chain delivered every new airframe onto its existing engines, with no development risk, while it funded durability upgrades.
  • Players read one disclosure in opposite ways. Boeing published an engine requirement ('launched by 2031, ready by 2038') without naming a maker. Rolls-Royce read it as an announcement and launched. Pratt & Whitney read it as no commitment and cancelled the engine Boeing then named.
  • Doctrine left value on the table. Airbus refused Delay Tactics on integrity grounds and gave up $0.7B in round 3, which would have kept its 60% edge. Boeing paid a $0.4B premium to take the rate step in the first clean round.
  • The open fan never flew. With NGSA (2028) and fps (2031) both committed on the LEAP derivative, no airframe remained for a 2045 RISE entry into service. CFM/GE called the objective unattainable rather than launch an engine with no airplane.

Round 1 (to 2030): positioning and first commitments (2026–2030)

Supply-chain crunch: running development programmes concurrently costs 50% more from now on. Airbus launched NGSA in 2028 and asked for UltraFan. Rolls-Royce held to 'no airframe, no engine', and CFM/GE preferred its LEAP derivative to a new engine, so NGSA locked onto the LEAP derivative (entry into service 2035). Boeing kept fps back behind its readiness and balance-sheet gates, and deferred the rate step under the crunch. Pratt & Whitney hedged with a next-generation GTF launched in 2030. All three engine makers funded durability upgrades (Trent 1000, GTF, LEAP). Airbus began public Poaching.

Method

How this was played and scored

  • Each player is an independent agent that reads only its own company and executive profiles and its own engine view; a runtime hook enforces this. The game master alone sees the full game-theory board and relays only public moves, statements and chosen disclosures.
  • Orders are sealed and simultaneous each round. Engine makers' orders apply first, so an airframer can pick an engine its maker commits to in the same round.
  • Every number is computed by the war-game engine (wargame/model.py). Volumes are stylised (2,000 narrowbodies and 170 widebodies a year); per-unit values are calibrated from the evidence, and parameters marked PLACEHOLDER in the config are assumptions.
