Boeing supply curves in the game

The fps ramps on the NGSA curve of your Airbus Rev. B chart, moved to the fps's entry into service: 1, 2, 8, 24, 46, 67, 80 a month in its first 7 years. The curve goes on to 92 and 100, but the fps is held to 90 a month until 2050 and runs at 100 from 2051. The 7-year and 10-year options share that ramp; they differ only in when the fps enters service (7 or 10 years after launch). As the fps ramps, the 737 MAX gives way, keeping a tail of 10 a month until 2050, so Boeing never builds more than 100 a month in total. Shown for a launch in 2030, the game's first decision year.

The rules behind the curves

  • 737 MAX. 42.5, 49.5, 52, 54.5 a month in 2026-2029, then 57. With no fps it carries on at 57 a month. The 737 rate increase (+100 aircraft a year from 2032) is not shown; it could be ordered only in 2030.
  • fps. Annual output on the NGSA curve of your chart (rate 50 about four years after entry into service, 100 about eight years after), moved to the fps date. Until 2050 it is held to 90 a month so the MAX keeps its tail; from 2051 the MAX ends and the fps runs at 100.
  • New sites (default). The MAX carries on at Renton and Everett and gives way only to Boeing's 100-a-month total.
  • Renton reassigned. Renton's MAX lines (47 a month) wind down as the fps ramps there, on the shape of the A320's base rundown on your chart; the Everett North Line carries on at up to 15 a month. This saves about $3.0B of new-site capex, net of the cost of converting Renton (range $1.5B-$5.5B), pro-rated if Renton is chosen less than three years before entry into service.

These are capacities; deliveries fall below them when demand does. In the game Boeing did not launch the fps, and its deliveries fell below the 57-a-month MAX-only capacity in 2037-2046, while NGSA entered service and ramped up.
