The war game with supply and demand

We replayed the dash-2050-obj war game (ExCo and Board agents, Control's objectives, rounds 2030, 2035, 2045 and 2050) with one change: an aircraft is sold only when a customer wants it and a factory can build it. Demand = replacement + growth: replacement of today's 737 MAX and A320neo fleets from your Cirium page, plus fleet growth, which your data did not give, from Airbus's and Boeing's market forecasts. Supply is your Airbus Rev. B plan and Boeing's plan: the 737 MAX, built to 2050 if an fps replaces it, and the fps at new sites or at Renton. Boeing never launched the fps. Airbus's NGSA still paid, but at about 29% of its value on the dashboard alone. Both makers were sold out through 2036 while they cleared the firm backlog; from 2046, new demand alone exceeds both makers' capacity.

Boeing never launched the fps. In 2030 the 10-year fps was worth +$4.30B against Do Nothing on the dashboard alone, but −$2.74B once deliveries are limited (−$2.19B at Renton). The dashboard books the fps's margin on every Boeing narrowbody from the day it enters service: about $14M of profit an aircraft, against about $4M for a 737 MAX. Here the fps ramps on the NGSA curve of your Rev. B chart: 8, 27, 93, 293, 551 aircraft in its first five years. So in those five years it earns $2.3B (at 2026) of the $9.6B the dashboard books for them, and its yearly margin catches up with the dashboard's only in 2049. The early margin is never made up. That cuts the fps's discounted narrowbody gain from $23.9B to $16.9B. The better 10-year fps was then worth −$2.19B in 2030 (Renton), −$0.75B in 2035 (Renton), +$0.11B in 2045 (new sites) and +$0.24B in 2050 (new sites). About half of that rise is only discounting, because a later launch's loss is smaller at 2026. Like for like, rising demand added about $0.58B, $0.39B and $0.18B a round. It never came near the +$1B that Boeing's Board requires of a launch in the most likely case.
NGSA still paid, but at about 29% of its dashboard value. Airbus ends at +$13.25B, against +$45.82B for the same moves on the dashboard alone (the supply-and-demand line is −$32.25B and the Alt plan's cost −$0.32B). Two things cut it. First, NGSA ramps on your chart (8, 27, 93, 293, 551 aircraft in its first five years), while the dashboard books its margin on every Airbus narrowbody from entry into service. Second, the dashboard's share rule moves Airbus from 60% to 80% of 2,000 aircraft a year (1,600) by 2043, but its factories settle at 100 a month, 1,200, once the A320 ends (briefly 1,266 in 2044, while Alt-plan A320s overlap NGSA). Airbus sells every aircraft it can build from 2038. NGSA reaches 100 a month in 2045, as the last Alt-plan A320s end, and builds 1,200 a year from 2046. The Alt plan was worth +$2.72B net of its $0.75B cost. Without fleet growth, NGSA would be worth −$0.80B.
Boeing loses less than the dashboard says, mostly thanks to Airbus's overflow. The dashboard's share rule takes Boeing's preferred share from 40% to 20% by 2043, falling 3 points a year from NGSA's entry into service in 2037. In a short market about half of Airbus's waiting customers are flexible and take Boeing's free slots: 4,390 aircraft over 2026-2060. So Boeing delivers 36% of the market from 2047. The supply-and-demand line adds +$2.87B to Boeing (its ΔPV is −$0.77B, against −$3.64B on the dashboard alone). About $1.71B of that is Airbus's overflow: with no flexible customers Boeing would be at −$2.47B. The rest comes from the status quo being capped too, since the 737 MAX builds 684 a year, not the dashboard's 800. The cost falls in the NGSA ramp years: in 2037-2045 Boeing delivers 4,631 aircraft, against 5,595 in the status quo.
The largest money left on the table was the 787 Re-engine. It was part of Boeing's best alternative in every round, worth +$3.30B in 2030 and +$0.54B by 2050 (by then together with a 10-year fps). In 2030 and 2035 Boeing held it because its Board would have vetoed it: it lost more than the Board's $1B limit if Airbus re-engined the A350 too. In 2045 and 2050 it was worth less than the +$1B the Board requires. Airbus left the A350 Re-engine (+$1.75B in 2030). In 2030 and 2035 its spending would have exceeded one year's free cash flow during NGSA's development. In 2045 and 2050 its CFO's robustness test stopped it inside the ExCo (it lost money if Boeing re-engined the 787), as did the company's own +$1B launch test, though its Board would have approved it.
By 2050 the single-aisle market is short of capacity, and no order adds more. In 2050 demand is 2,307 aircraft against 1,884 of capacity. Waiting customers reach 1,124 in 2050 and 2,865 by 2060, and 5,378 customers drop out over 2026-2060 (2,301 of them in 2026-2036, while both makers were sold out). After 2030 no order raises an existing line's rate: the 737 rate increase could be ordered only in 2030, and Boeing did not order it. New capacity can come only from a new programme such as the fps, which would lift Boeing to 100 a month. Airbus's ExCo said it was preparing a capacity step but did not commit it.
The engine makers settled early. Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce committed to the Joint Venture in 2035, folding GTF2 and UltraFan NB into it. In the same sealed round CFM launched its ducted engine and the Embraer partnership. With no fps, the 737 stays CFM-only, so Boeing misses its dual-source objective. CFM ends at −$23.47B, Rolls-Royce at +$9.61B and Pratt & Whitney at −$0.48B. Most of the change from the previous game (−$69.69B, +$32.35B, +$8.88B) comes from engines now selling only on aircraft actually delivered, not from the missing fps (see Against the previous game).

What we added: supply and demand

The dashboard sells a fixed 2,000 narrowbodies a year, split by its share rules, and books every one. The supply-and-demand layer keeps the dashboard's shares as what customers prefer, and delivers only what the factories can build. The firm backlog is delivered first, then new demand, then customers still waiting from earlier years. Flexible customers take the other maker's free slots when their first choice is full, and a tenth of those still waiting drop out each year.

Equilibria and regrets

The round game is the airframers' options for that round, with everyone else's orders as played and no later moves. Neither airframer played a pure equilibrium of the round: each side's best reply included a Re-engine that its own tests ruled out. Regret is the final ΔPV of a side's best alternative minus what it played, with everyone else's actual orders and later rounds replayed.

Method and caveats

The game. Run dash-2050-sd: your combined_dash.py dashboard (v3), run by the game master without its interface, with its adjustments, Control's objectives and the supply-and-demand layer. There were four sealed rounds (2030, 2035, 2045, 2050), each company deciding through its ExCo and Board agents. Each player saw only its own brief and the public bulletin. An audit of every file and tool output each agent touched found all four rounds clean (one false alarm in 2035, a fault in the audit script, was fixed).

A correction after play. After the game, a check found that growth had been reduced by the A220 share twice instead of once, so the players were briefed on growth about 5% too low (new demand about 4% too low over 2026-2060). This page uses the corrected demand. We re-valued every round's options on it, with everyone else's orders as played. Each player's best option is the same as when it decided, except that in 2050 Boeing's best option is the 10-year fps at new sites with the 787 Re-engine rather than the 10-year fps at Renton with the 787 Re-engine. No decision changes against the players' own tests. The largest shift for Boeing's fps is in 2030: the 10-year fps at Renton was −$2.73B in the brief and is −$2.19B now, still far below its Board's +$1B.

The layer. New demand (replacement + growth) is split by the dashboard's share as preference. The firm backlog is delivered first, then new demand plus customers still waiting, up to each maker's capacity. Flexible customers (48.7%, from your page's customers flying both types, lessors and unannounced customers) move to the other maker's free slots, and 10% of those still waiting drop out each year. Engine makers sell on the aircraft actually delivered: the 737 MAX on CFM, the A320neo on CFM and P&W (60/40), and the new types on their chosen engines. With unlimited capacity and demand of 2,000 a year, the layer gives back the dashboard's airframer values exactly (tested).

What is ours, not yours. Growth, the firm-backlog treatment, the flexible share window, the drop-out rate (not calibrated), Boeing's 737 MAX path and its 100-a-month cap with a 10-a-month MAX tail to 2050 once an fps is in service, the fps ramp on the NGSA curve, and the Renton figures. Renton runs at 47 a month and Everett at up to 15; the new-site saving is $3.0B, pro-rated if Renton is chosen less than three years before entry into service. The sources are in wargame/dashgame/supply_demand_sources.md.
