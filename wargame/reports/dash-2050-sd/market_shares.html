Narrowbody market shares: Do Nothing and fps

The fps business case rests on two shares. The first is an assumption: the share of new 737/A320-class orders customers want to give Boeing, set by the dashboard's share rule. The second is the outcome: Boeing's share of what is actually delivered, once the game has served the firm backlog and limited each maker to what it can build. With Do Nothing, customers' preference for Boeing falls to 20%, but Boeing still delivers 36% of the market, because Airbus cannot build the rest. With the fps, preference recovers to 47% and deliveries follow it. Both cases: Airbus's 2030 orders as played (NGSA in service 2037, A320 Alt plan); the fps launched in 2030, in service 2040, at Renton.

The assumption: customer preference

Until a new narrowbody enters service, customers split 40/60 between Boeing and Airbus. NGSA enters service in 2037. With Do Nothing, Airbus then gains 3 points a year until it holds 80%. With the fps, Airbus gains only 1 point a year while NGSA is alone, and from 2040 Boeing wins back 1 point a year until it reaches 47% in 2049.

Settings and where they come from

  • Status quo: Boeing 40%, Airbus 60% until the first new narrowbody enters service. Dashboard constant.
  • NGSA alone (Boeing does not launch): Airbus +3 points a year to 80%. Fixed in the dashboard, not a slider (an fps alone would instead climb at the Boeing capture speed below). Here: Boeing 37% in 2037, falling to 20% from 2043.
  • Both launch: the leader's edge, 1 point a year of lead (slider "NB share edge per year of EIS lead (pp)", 1.0). While NGSA is alone in service, Airbus gains 1 point a year: Boeing 39%, 38%, 37% in 2037-2039.
  • Long-run split: the balance point less the leader's edge. Balance point 50/50 (slider "Boeing fps share of post-2037 NB market (%)", 50), shifted 1 point per year of lead toward the first new narrowbody in service: 50% − 1 × 3 = 47% for Boeing.
  • Boeing's capture speed: 1 point a year (slider "Boeing share capture (pp/yr) — 10-yr ramp", 1.0; "fps capture speed" in the snapshot): Boeing climbs from 37% in 2039 to 47% in 2049.

Source: board v3 (combined_dash.py, the unified narrowbody share rule) and the Game v3 snapshot. The dashboard alone sells these shares of a fixed 2,000 aircraft a year; the game uses them to split real demand.

The outcome: what Boeing delivers

The supply-and-demand layer turns preference into deliveries. Each year the firm backlog is delivered first (737 MAX 4,769, A320neo family 7,330 at the start). New demand (replacement plus fleet growth, 752 aircraft in 2030 and 2,307 in 2050) is split by preference. Each maker delivers what it can build: 48.7% of customers it cannot serve take the other maker's free slots, and 10% of those still waiting drop out each year.

Do Nothing: customers want 20% Boeing, but Boeing delivers 36%

Delivered sharePreferred share

From 2038 Airbus sells all it can build: 807-843 a year in 2038-2040 while it switches from the A320 to NGSA, and at most 1,200 a year from 2046 (NGSA at 100 a month). In 2050, 1,845 new customers want an Airbus and 826 are still waiting from earlier years. Airbus builds 1,200, 223 take every 737 MAX slot Boeing has free, 125 drop out, and 1,124 are still waiting at year end (2,865 by 2060). Boeing sells all 684 MAX it can build from 2047. Before 2037 both makers sell out, so delivered shares follow capacity, not preference. In 2037 neither sells out: Boeing delivers its last 241 waiting customers on top of 432 new ones (673 of 684), while Airbus, with only 175 customers still waiting, delivers 911 of 1,006. Airbus delivering less is what lifts Boeing's delivered share to its 42.5% peak.

fps: deliveries follow preference once the fps is at rate

Delivered sharePreferred share

In 2041-2043 Boeing delivers less than customers want (512-577 aircraft against 589-698 wanted): the fps is still ramping up and Renton's 737 MAX lines are winding down. From 2050 NGSA's customers overflow to Boeing, so Boeing delivers slightly more than its preference. In 2050 they get 737 MAX (15), because the fps line is held at 1,080 a year while the last MAX are built at Everett; from 2051 they get the fps.

Notes

  • Why this matters for the business case. On the dashboard alone, the long-run gap in preference (20% against 47%) is 540 aircraft a year of the fixed 2,000. In the game the 2050 gap is 411 aircraft (684 against 1,095): Do Nothing keeps more than its preference because Airbus's overflow buys 737 MAX, which is the Do Nothing benefit the business case nets out.
  • The three years before the fps flies. The fps case is ahead in preference from 2037 only because the rule slows Airbus from 3 points a year to 1 once Boeing has launched. That earns the fps case $0.35B of operating profit in 2037-2039 ($0.10B at 2026) before a single fps is delivered. It is a dashboard assumption, not a market observation.
  • What is the dashboard's and what is ours. The share rule and its settings are the dashboard's. Demand, the firm backlog, capacities, the overflow and drop-out rates are game-master assumptions (supply_demand.json and supply_demand_sources.md).
