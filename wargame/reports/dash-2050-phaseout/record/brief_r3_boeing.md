## Public statements (last round)

- **Boeing:** Five years ago I said we'd get the basics right first, and the team has kept at it. The 737 line is steady at 57 a month, the 777X and the 737-7 and -10 are in service, and the balance sheet is where we said we'd put it. That's the base we build the future on.

On the 737, we're staying at 57 a month. Our KPIs are stable. Rates stay tied to the data, and we agree them with the FAA, the same way we always have.

On widebodies, the 787 and 777X lines are stable, and we'll keep putting our effort into the airplanes our customers fly today.

On a new single-aisle airplane, we've been doing the work on three fronts: the market, the technology and our own readiness. Two of those have come a long way. The engine technology is maturing, and our balance sheet is ready. The business case isn't there yet, and I'm not going to launch an airplane until it is. We'll do a new airplane when the market and the technology and we're ready. I'm not going to give you a date. When we do launch, the airplane will carry more than one engine source, and the entry-into-service date will have headroom built in.

In the meantime we're keeping our engineering team and our supply base ready, so that when the time comes we can execute. It is so much more important that we do this right than fast.
- **Airbus:** Our core principles of safety, quality, integrity, compliance and security will remain at the heart of all we do. They frame every step our next-generation single-aisle aircraft takes towards entry into service.

The programme remains in development, and we continue to plan its entry into service in the second half of the 2030s. Its timing follows a technology and production system we have been maturing, and we keep it on our own clock. We will report progress against milestones, in windows, and we will not trade safety or quality for speed.

We are preparing the Airbus of the 2030s while we keep delivering for our customers today. The A320 family stays at its present rate until the new aircraft enters service, and our production plan keeps A320 lines running longer through the new aircraft's ramp-up. The transition will be disciplined and controlled, rate by rate, with quality first: each step is taken only when the quality indicators for it are met. We will certify the aircraft's engines from CFM, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce in sequence, as each one matures.

The programme remains funded within our cash flow and preserves our financial flexibility and balance-sheet discipline; the case assumes no state support. We continue to secure our own supply base, from work packages to materials, and to invest in the people who will design and build this aircraft. We will keep working with our industry partners for a tariff-free, zero-for-zero regime for civil aerospace.
- **CFM/GE:** Safety, quality, delivery and cost, always in that order. That hasn't changed, and it won't.

Let me tell you what we know and what we don't. What we know: Airbus has launched its new single-aisle airplane and asked CFM to be on it. We don't have a birthright on that order. We'll earn it on the product and on durability. So CFM, with our partner Safran, is launching a new ducted engine for the airplanes that chose CFM, and we'll offer it to both airframers. It builds on the LEAP our customers fly today and carries what we've learned in RISE. I'll be plain about what it is and what it isn't. It's the right engine for these airplanes. It isn't the next generation. That's the open fan.

We're also going to work with Embraer to bring a CFM engine to its airplanes. That adds to what we do for our two big customers, and it doesn't take anything away from them.

What we don't know yet is the date. You'll get one when our testing backs it, and not before. Durability comes ahead of the date: if the testing needs more time, it gets it. And job one hasn't moved. We keep LEAP and GEnx engines on wing longer for the customers flying them today.

RISE stays a technology program, and we're all in on it with Safran. On the testing we have today, an open fan comes ten years from a launch with an airframer, and not before 2045. It launches with an airframer, not ahead of one.

It's that simple, ain't that hard.
- **Pratt & Whitney:** The bad news first. The GTF fleet still has work ahead of it, and that work comes before anything new: time on wing, parts, and our customers' aircraft back in the air. The GTF Advantage hot section keeps going into the fleet at shop visits, and we will keep doing it until the fleet is where it needs to be. Control what you can control.

GTF2 is in its last year of development and is due to be ready in 2036, six years from launch. Durability will be proven before entry into service, with every life-limited part at full life on day one. If the testing needs more time, the testing gets it. We have learned our lesson from the prior iterations of the GTF. We offer an engine to an airframer only where it can be ready by that airframe's entry into service.

Today Pratt & Whitney puts on the record its commitment to a Joint Venture with Rolls-Royce on the next narrowbody engine. A Joint Venture spreads risk and investment; it does not share technology. Our gear and the GTF Advantage core come in as Pratt & Whitney modules, designed and built by Pratt & Whitney, and they stay ours. The partners hold equal shares, and each partner's own development work counts toward its own share. No partner buys the other's technology. GTF2 is what we bring. Rolls-Royce speaks for itself, and the Joint Venture forms only when both partners have committed.

We compete on standard terms, on the product and on the durability record. We're not in the early entry phase, the launch phase. The airframers speak for their own programmes and dates, and we will not get out ahead of them. These are responsible bets with responsible paybacks.
- **Rolls-Royce:** So, UltraFan for the narrowbody is in development on its own clock. Entry into service no earlier than 2037, and only when it is mature. We don't shorten testing to meet a date. Our preference is partnership. Rolls-Royce has made a standing commitment to a Joint Venture with Pratt & Whitney, on standard terms that credit the technology we bring. We don't need it for capability. That changes our line, not our strategy. Widebody is our core, and our focus there stays on time on wing. We won't do anything not profitable. I will say that categorically.
