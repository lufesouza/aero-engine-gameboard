## Public statements (last round)

- **Boeing:** Ten years ago we said we'd get the basics right first, and we have. The balance sheet is where we said we'd put it, and it has stayed there. The 777X and the 737-7 and -10 are in service, and the 787 and 777X lines are stable.

First, a correction. Last time I said the 737 line was steady at 57 a month. That number is behind us now, and I'd rather fix it than leave it out there. The 737 MAX is in the later part of its life. We'll build it at the pace our customers need, and we'll step it down the same way we stepped it up: one step at a time, on the data, agreed with the FAA. Quality comes ahead of the schedule on the way down, just as it did on the way up. And every MAX in service will have our full support for as long as it flies.

On a new single-aisle airplane, how we decide hasn't changed. We look at three things: the market, the technology and our own readiness. The technology is there, and so is our balance sheet. The business case isn't, and I'm not going to launch an airplane until it is. I'm not going to give you a date, and I'd rather tell you that plainly than sell you a promise I can't keep.

One more correction, on engines. When we do launch, the airplane will carry more than one engine source, from engines that are ready when the airplane is. That's the requirement. I'm not going to name makers today, and the entry-into-service date will have headroom built in.

In the meantime we're keeping our engineering team and our supply base ready, so that when the time is right we can execute. It is so much more important that we do this right than fast.
- **Airbus:** Our core principles of safety, quality, integrity, compliance and security will remain at the heart of all we do. Our new single-aisle aircraft has been in service since 2037 and is now in a disciplined and controlled ramp-up: rate by rate, quality first, with each step taken only when our quality and supply-chain indicators are met. The last aircraft of the A320 family will leave our lines over the coming period, supported to the end with the same care our customers have known for decades. In widebodies, the A350 family, including the A350F, remains our offer. We continue to assess its evolution as engine technology matures, and we will decide on our own timetable and within our financial discipline. Our programmes are funded from our own cash flow, which preserves our financial flexibility. We are preparing the next generation of aircraft through our technology roadmap with our partners, on technology readiness, not on a calendar. We will keep working with our industry partners for a tariff-free, zero-for-zero regime in civil aerospace.
- **CFM/GE:** Safety, quality, delivery and cost, always in that order. That hasn't changed, and it won't.

Let me tell you what we know and what we don't. What we know: the new single-aisle airplane is flying, and CFM is on it with our partner Safran. Our new ducted engine is in service on the airplanes that chose it. It's the right engine for those airplanes, and we offer it to both airframers. It isn't the next generation. That's the open fan. Our work with Embraer goes on too. It adds to what we do for our two big customers and takes nothing away from them.

So job one this year is the fleet. As airlines change over from today's airplanes to the new ones, LEAP, ducted and GEnx engines have to stay on wing. The kits and spares they need get first call, and spares never crowd out the new engines our customers are waiting on. We'll tell you how the fleet is doing when the field data shows it, and not before.

RISE stays a technology program, and we're all in on it with Safran. On the testing we have today, an open fan comes no sooner than ten years from a launch with an airframer. It launches with an airframer, not ahead of one. When that is, it's really not for us to say.

What we don't know is when the next new airplane comes, or what it will ask of us. We don't have a birthright on that order. When it comes, we'll compete for it on the product and on durability.

We're not going for share for its own sake. We get paid in dollars, not in percentages. The balance sheet stays strong, and we keep returning cash to our shareholders.

It's that simple, ain't that hard.
- **Pratt & Whitney:** The bad news first. The move from one generation of single-aisle aircraft to the next cost us volume. Our share of narrowbody engines dipped in 2039 and 2040 before it came back to about a quarter. Our competitors answered with new engines of their own, as they should, and what we have earned on the money we spent to get here has come in short of what we planned. We own that, and we are not going to dress it up.

The GTF fleet still flying on the A320neo comes first: time on wing, parts, and our customers' aircraft back in the air. The GTF Advantage hot section keeps going into that fleet at shop visits, and new production never crowds out its spares. Control what you can control.

The Joint Venture with Rolls-Royce formed in 2035, when both partners had committed. Its engine, built on GTF2, has been ready since 2036 and is in service on NGSA. Our gear and the GTF Advantage core are Pratt & Whitney modules, designed and built by Pratt & Whitney, and they stay ours. Rolls-Royce speaks for itself. Any change to the engine in service comes only after its own durability campaign is complete. If the testing needs more time, the testing gets it. We have learned our lesson from the prior iterations of the GTF.

We offer the Joint Venture engine to an airframer only where it is ready by that airframe's entry into service, and it is ready now. We compete on standard terms, on the product and on the durability record. We're not in the early entry phase, the launch phase. The airframers speak for their own programmes, dates and engine choices, and we will not get out ahead of them. The development spend is behind us. What we control now is how this engine runs in service.
- **Rolls-Royce:** So, our Joint Venture engine with Pratt & Whitney is in service. Our narrowbody programme folded into the Joint Venture in 2035, and that replaces our earlier line on UltraFan for the narrowbody. Our narrowbody development is complete. Our focus now is maturity and time on wing, and they come first on every fleet we power, narrowbody and Trent. We offer the engine on standard terms, jointly with our partner. Widebody is our core, and we defend it through time on wing. Frankly, we don't commit that easily. We won't do anything not profitable. I will say that categorically.
