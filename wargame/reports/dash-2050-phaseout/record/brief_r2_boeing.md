## Public statements (last round)

- **Boeing:** We said we'd get the basics right first, and the team has done that. The 777X is delivering, the 737-7 and -10 are in service, and we've paid the debt down to where we wanted it. I think we've turned the ship. The job now is to keep it pointed the right way.

On the 737, we're staying at 57 a month. Our KPIs are stable, and that rate serves the demand we see. We'll move the rate when the demand and the data say so, and not before.

On widebodies, the 777X is in service and the 787 line is stable at Charleston. We're going to keep both of those lines running the way they are now.

On a new single-aisle airplane, we're doing the work on the market, the technology and our own readiness. We'll do a new airplane when the market and the technology and we're ready. We're not there yet, and I'm not going to give you a date. When we do it, we want more than one engine source on that airplane. That's a requirement, and we're working on it with the engine makers.

It's so much more important that we do this right than fast.
- **Airbus:** Our core principles of safety, quality, integrity, compliance and security will remain at the heart of all we do, and they frame today's decision. Airbus is launching its next-generation single-aisle aircraft. It is planned to enter service in the second half of the 2030s. The date follows a technology and production system we have matured over the past decade, and we set it on our own clock.

We are preparing the Airbus of the 2030s while we keep delivering for our customers today. The A320 family stays at its present rate until the new aircraft enters service. Our production plan then keeps A320 lines running longer through the new aircraft's ramp-up, so that the transition is disciplined and controlled, rate by rate, with quality first. We have asked CFM, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce to propose engines for the aircraft, and we will certify them in sequence as each one matures.

The programme is funded within our cash flow and preserves our financial flexibility and balance-sheet discipline; the case assumes no state support. We are securing our own supply base, from work packages to materials, and investing in the people who will design and build this aircraft. We will keep working with our industry partners for a tariff-free, zero-for-zero regime for civil aerospace. We will report progress against milestones, in windows, as the aircraft matures.
- **CFM/GE:** Safety, quality, delivery and cost, always in that order. That's how we'll run this business through the next decade.

Let me tell you what we know and what we don't. What we know: our customers fly more LEAP and GEnx hours every year, and job one is keeping those engines on wing longer. So we keep putting durability upgrades into the LEAP fleet, and we're upgrading the GEnx for the 787 operators who count on it.

What we don't know is when the next single-aisle airplanes come. It's really not for us to say. That's the airframers' call, shaped by the airlines. When they decide, CFM will be there for both of them, with the engine their airplane needs, LEAP-based or new, on a date our testing backs. We don't have a birthright on that next order. We'll earn it.

RISE stays a technology program, and we're all in on it with our partner Safran. On the testing we have today, an open fan would not enter service before 2045, ten years from a launch with an airframer. It launches with an airframer, not ahead of one.

It's that simple, ain't that hard.
- **Pratt & Whitney:** The bad news first. The GTF fleet still has work ahead of it, and that work comes before anything new: time on wing, parts, and getting our customers' aircraft back in the air. We are putting the GTF Advantage hot section into the fleet at shop visits, and we will keep doing it until the fleet is where it needs to be. Control what you can control.

Here is our commitment on the next engine. Pratt & Whitney will launch its next-generation geared turbofan, GTF2, in any round an airframer selects it. It will be ready six years from launch, which means 2036 if it is launched this year. Durability will be proven before entry into service, with every life-limited part at full life on day one. GTF2 builds on the gear and the GTF Advantage core that are flying today. We have learned our lesson from the prior iterations of the GTF. If the testing needs more time, the testing gets it.

We will compete on standard terms, on the product and on the durability record. We're not in the early entry phase, the launch phase. The airframers speak for their own programmes and dates, and we will not get out ahead of them. When we've seen the demand and when we feel good about it, we invest. These are responsible bets with responsible paybacks.
- **Rolls-Royce:** So, our position is simple. Rolls-Royce will launch UltraFan for a narrowbody in the round an airframer selects it, and not before. Entry into service no earlier than seven years from launch. We don't commit that easily: until I am sure we will deliver, I'm not committing. Our preference is partnership. The Joint Venture is open to Pratt & Whitney on terms that credit the technology we bring, and we will commit once they do. Widebody is our core, and our focus there stays on time on wing. We won't do anything not profitable. I will say that categorically.
