What staying in the narrowbody market is worth, and whether it changes the fps decision

Boeing's brief counts an fps for 20 years from entry into service and Do Nothing to 2060. It counts nothing after that, and it never prices what leaving the narrowbody market costs. This page builds that missing piece from how Boeing has valued its franchises since 2003 and from how analysts, Airbus and companies that left their markets value them. It adds five terms to the brief, re-runs Boeing's decision rule with them, and reports the result at the game's 10.5% and at 8.1% (10.5% read as a nominal rate, less 2.2% inflation).

Basis. Constant 2026 $B, pre-tax, discounted to 2026, against Do Nothing, in the columns the CEO expected each round (2030: Airbus launches NGSA). Rivals' moves are held as the game played them: this is an overlay on Boeing's valuation, not a replay of the game. With the terms switched off, the model gives back the brief's value of every plan in every column to the cent, and every decision threshold on the main report.

The answer

The franchise is worth about a third of the fps's gap to Do Nothing In 2030 the terms add +$2.30B to the 10-year fps at Renton and +$3.29B to the 7-year. The brief has them $6.37B and $8.71B behind Do Nothing; with the franchise they are still $4.07B and $5.42B behind. Continuing value is the biggest term (+$1.49B for the 10-year).
At the game's 10.5%, no case changes any decision The margin Boeing needs falls in every round: 2030 from 36.6–39.9% to 32.4–35.4% (high case 29.7–31.0%), 2035 to 38.7%. It never reaches 25.64%. 2045 and 2050 stay out of reach (82.0% and 161.1%).
The discount rate decides it Most of the franchise is earned after 2060, when a dollar is worth 3 cents today at 10.5%. At 8.1% the brief's own 2030 threshold drops to 31.8–32.9% and the franchise takes it to 26.9%. The fps then beats Do Nothing, but the 787 Re-engine alone is worth more, so the CEO's rule still picks the Re-engine. In the high case at 8.1%, Boeing launches: the 10-year fps on new sites in 2030 (and the 7-year at Renton in the 2035 brief). So does the base case if demand rises after 2061.
Waiting is where the franchise is lost At 8.1%, launching in 2030 is the only date that beats never launching (+$0.89B for the 10-year at Renton); from 2035 every launch is behind (−$2.00B in 2035, −$4.42B in 2045). At 10.5% the franchise terms shrink from +$2.30B for a 2030 launch to +$0.54B for 2045. Do Nothing in 2030 is a deferral, and the deferral is what costs the franchise.

How Boeing has valued staying in

Boeing has never left its core narrowbody market. It has left niches on a forward-looking business case, defended the core with the cheapest adequate product when share was at risk, and, in its own programme decisions, counted franchise value that the war game's brief leaves out.

How others value it

Analysts never stop at a programme's horizon

  • Morningstar runs an explicit forecast, a fade and a perpetuity, and gives wide-moat firms such as Boeing at least 20 years of excess returns. It estimates that "half of Boeing's enterprise value is derived from its 737 product line" (analyst estimate).
  • Barclays (2019) put the whole narrowbody industry at $238B of present value, about $2.4B per share point (analyst estimate).
  • McKinsey's value-driver formula and Mauboussin's competitive-advantage period are the standard tools: excess returns fade toward the cost of capital rather than stopping.

Airbus pays to stay in

  • The A320neo cost just over EUR 1bn and, in Leahy's words, left the CSeries "virtually no business case"; it has 11,529 orders.
  • When customers rejected the first A350, Airbus spent about EUR 11.6bn on the A350 XWB rather than cede the segment (EADS, 2006).
  • Faury on the next single-aisle: "If we move first, the supply chain will come to us."

Leaving is close to permanent

  • McDonnell Douglas took 3-4% of 1996 orders against a 12% ten-year average once it stopped launching products; the FTC found it "no longer ... a meaningful competitive force".
  • None of eight airframers that left large commercial aircraft since 1962 came back (Convair to Bombardier).
  • Exits cost one to three years of the segment's losses (Douglas $1.4B, Saab SKr4bn, BAE £400M), and the installed base outlives the exit: Rolls-Royce sold its IAE stake for $1.5bn plus flight-hour payments, and says its installed base "is where the majority of the value of the business lies".

Other industries say the same

  • Christensen and colleagues (HBR, 2008): the do-nothing case wrongly assumes "the present health of the company will persist".
  • Dixit (1989): exit and re-entry are hysteretic, so an exit is close to irreversible.
  • Intel left DRAM and GE applied "number one or two" only once the position was already lost and the capital had a better use; Boeing's narrowbody meets neither condition (inference).

The model: five terms added to the brief

Each term is a yearly flow for every plan in every column of every round, discounted like the brief and counted to the same end year within a round (the latest fps entry into service plus 50 years). After 2061, the game's own supply-and-demand engine is run on, with Boeing's preference held where the game left it. In this world the 737 MAX is gone from 2060 in every plan and in the status quo, so Do Nothing earns no narrowbody profit after 2060.

Two terms left out of the views, and what they would do

  • Overhead read the other way. If the game's margins are programme margins with no overhead in them, every plan carries the overhead it keeps, and an fps keeps all of it for 50 years. The term then works against the fps (−$0.67B for the 10-year in 2030, instead of +$0.36B), and the 2030 margin needed is 33.8–36.8%. The repo does not document the margins' basis; Boeing's programme accounting absorbs production overhead, so the fully loaded reading is the base.
  • The MAX conceding price against NGSA. 1.5% of the MAX price from 2037, full in Do Nothing, half on fps plans: +$0.75B for the 10-year in 2030. With it, the 2030 margin needed is 31.5–34.5% at 10.5% and 25.9% at 8.1%. It is left out because it conflicts with the game's own supply-and-demand state, which already sets the MAX's sales.

Notes

  • Not counted: anything after the fps's 50th year (no successor, no perpetuity), tax and working capital, a probability that the franchise case fails (the MAX grounding cost about $18.6B; a 20% failure chance would cut continuing value and services by a fifth), and the option to come back after leaving (valued at zero).
  • Overlay, not a replay: rivals do not react to Boeing's valuation, and the CEO's and the Board's rules (the $1B business-case test, the −$1B downside test, the $2B rating buffer and the $1B tie rule) are applied unchanged.
  • Derivative: the sustaining derivative is always bought. In the low case (25% of the bill) it can cost more than the late-life profit it sustains, which is why the low case slightly raises the 2045 and 2050 thresholds.
  • Rates: at 8.1% every line of the brief is re-discounted and the debt penalty re-priced at the new present value; the dashboard's debt logic is not re-run.
