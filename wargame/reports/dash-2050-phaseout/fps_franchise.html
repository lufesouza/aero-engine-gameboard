What staying in the narrowbody market is worth, and whether it changes the fps decision

Boeing's brief counts an fps for 20 years from entry into service and Do Nothing to 2060. It counts nothing after that, and it never prices what leaving the narrowbody market costs. This page builds that missing piece from how Boeing has valued its franchises since 2003 and from how analysts, Airbus and companies that left their markets value them. It adds five terms to the brief, re-runs Boeing's decision rule with them, and reports the result at the game's 10.5% and at 8.1% (10.5% read as a nominal rate, less 2.2% inflation).

Basis. Constant 2026 $B, pre-tax, discounted to 2026, against Do Nothing, in the columns the CEO expected each round (2030: Airbus launches NGSA). Rivals' moves are held as the game played them: this is an overlay on Boeing's valuation, not a replay of the game. With the terms switched off, the model gives back the brief's value of every plan in every column to the cent, and every decision threshold on the main report.

The answer

At the game's 10.5%, the franchise is worth about a third of the fps's gap to Do Nothing In 2030 the terms add +$2.30B to the 10-year fps at Renton and +$3.28B to the 7-year. The brief has them $6.37B and $8.71B behind Do Nothing; with the franchise they are still $4.07B and $5.43B behind. Continuing value is the biggest term (+$1.48B for the 10-year). At 8.1% the same terms (+$5.61B and +$7.52B) more than close the brief's gaps ($4.74B and $5.39B).
At the game's 10.5%, no case changes any decision The margin Boeing needs falls in every round: 2030 from 36.6–39.9% to 32.4–35.4% (high case 29.7–30.9%), 2035 to 38.7%. It never reaches 25.64%. 2045 and 2050 stay out of reach (69.1% and 95.7%). Most of that fall rests on holding demand up after 2060: on the run's own curve, which collapses after 2060, the margin needed is 97.0% and 209.6%. Holding demand lifts both the brief's own counted years that run past 2060 and the franchise terms after them: in 2045 the two are about equal (13 and 15 points); in 2050 the counted years are most of it (64 of 114 points).
At 8.1% the fps comes close; it launches only in the high case or if demand rises Most of the franchise is earned after 2060, when a dollar is worth 3 cents today at 10.5%. At 8.1% the brief's own 2030 threshold drops to 31.8–32.9% and the franchise takes it to 26.9%. The fps then beats Do Nothing, but the 787 Re-engine alone is worth more, so the CEO picks the Re-engine; the Board vetoes it on its downside test (−$2.63B in the two A350 Re-engine columns), as it did in the game's 2030 round at 10.5% (−$2.48B), and Boeing does nothing. In the high case, or if demand rises after 2060, the CEO picks the 10-year fps on new sites with the Re-engine; the Board vetoes the Re-engine beside it (about −$4.2B in the A350 Re-engine column) and the fps goes ahead alone, at +$7.1B against Do Nothing in the high case and +$3.1B with rising demand. The high case also launches the 10-year at Renton in the 2035 brief.
At 8.1%, only a 2030 launch beats never launching With the franchise, the 10-year at Renton is +$0.86B against Do Nothing launched in 2030, −$1.75B in 2035 and −$3.11B in 2045. Delay costs volume: its 20 counted years deliver 11,619 fps for a 2030 launch, 8,642 for 2035 and 4,684 for 2045, mostly because a later fps sells its mature years into the run's falling market (about 1,933 a year in 2050, 1,106 in 2060, where the base holds it), and partly at a lower share (Boeing's preference at the end of the game's years is 47%, 42% and 32%). At 10.5% discounting outweighs that: each delay makes the fps less bad (−$4.07B, −$3.54B, −$2.25B) but never good, while the margin it needs rises (32.4–35.4% in 2030, 38.7% in 2035). The franchise terms themselves fall from +$2.30B to +$0.60B between a 2030 and a 2045 launch, about what fifteen more years of discounting give.

How Boeing has valued staying in

Boeing has never left its core narrowbody market. It has left niches on a forward-looking business case, defended the core with the cheapest adequate product when share was at risk, and, in its own programme decisions, counted franchise value that the war game's brief leaves out.

How others value it

Analysts never stop at a programme's horizon

  • Morningstar runs an explicit forecast, a fade and a perpetuity, and gives wide-moat firms such as Boeing at least 20 years of excess returns. It estimates that "half of Boeing's enterprise value is derived from its 737 product line" (analyst estimate).
  • Barclays (2019) put the whole narrowbody industry at $238B of present value, about $2.4B per share point (analyst estimate).
  • McKinsey's value-driver formula and Mauboussin's competitive-advantage period are the standard tools: excess returns fade toward the cost of capital rather than stopping.

Airbus pays to stay in

  • Airbus launched the A320neo for just over EUR 1bn of investment and, in Leahy's words, left the CSeries "virtually no business case"; it has 11,529 orders.
  • When customers rejected the first A350, Airbus committed about EUR 11.6bn to the A350 XWB (EADS launch budget, 2006) rather than cede the segment.
  • Faury on the next single-aisle: "If we move first, the supply chain will come to us."

Leaving is close to permanent

  • McDonnell Douglas took 3-4% of 1996 world orders, against a 12% ten-year average share in the EEA (EC M.877), once it stopped developing new large aircraft (the MD-12 found no buyers and the MD-XX was dropped in 1996); the FTC found it "no longer ... a meaningful competitive force".
  • None of eight airliner makers that left the market since 1962 came back: three large-jet makers (Convair, Lockheed, Douglas) and five regional and small-jet makers (Fokker to Bombardier).
  • Exits cost more than a year's losses (Saab's SKr4bn after-tax provision was about four years of its average loss; Douglas $1.4B pre-tax; BAE £250M for the RJ, plus £150M for other commercial activities), and the installed base outlives the exit: Rolls-Royce sold its IAE stake for $1.5bn plus flight-hour payments, and says its installed base "is where the majority of the value of the business lies".

Other industries say the same

  • Christensen and colleagues (HBR, 2008): the do-nothing case wrongly assumes "the present health of the company will persist".
  • Dixit (1989): exit and re-entry are hysteretic (firms stay in below breakeven and come back only well above it); with a re-entry cost of at least the fps bill, Boeing's exit would be close to irreversible (inference).
  • Intel left DRAM and GE applied "number one or two" only once the position was already lost and the capital had a better use; Boeing's narrowbody meets neither condition (inference).

The model: five terms added to the brief

Each term is a yearly flow for every plan in every column of every round, discounted like the brief and counted to the same end year within a round (the latest fps's 50th year in service). After 2060, every year, counted by the brief or not, comes from the game's own supply-and-demand engine run on one of three demand tails, with Boeing's preference held where the game left it, so each tail is one consistent world. In this world the 737 MAX is gone from 2060 in every plan and in the status quo, so Do Nothing sells no narrowbodies after 2060 (services on the MAX fleet it has delivered still count, under Services).

Views used below: core is continuing value, services and closing costs (with the brief's counted years after 2060 put on the same demand, which is zero for a 2030 launch); then + overhead adds stranded overhead; then + widebody adds widebody erosion (judgement). The high and low cases move every parameter together toward or against the fps, except closing costs.

Two terms left out of the views, and what they would do

  • Overhead read the other way. If the game's margins are programme margins with no overhead in them, every plan carries the overhead it keeps, and an fps keeps all of it for 50 years. The term then works against the fps (−$0.67B for the 10-year in 2030, instead of +$0.36B), and with all other terms on the 2030 margin needed is 33.6–36.6% instead of 32.4–35.4%. The repo does not document the margins' basis; Boeing's programme accounting absorbs production overhead, so the fully loaded reading is the base.
  • The MAX conceding price against NGSA. 1.5% of the MAX price from 2037, full in Do Nothing, half on fps plans: +$0.75B for the 10-year in 2030. Added to all the other terms, the 2030 margin needed is 31.5–34.6% at 10.5% and 26.0% at 8.1%. It is left out because it conflicts with the game's own supply-and-demand state, which already sets the MAX's sales.

Notes

  • Not counted: deliveries after the fps's 50th year in service (no successor, no perpetuity; services on aircraft already delivered, and the final closing cost, still count), tax and working capital, a probability that the franchise case fails (the MAX grounding cost about $18.6B on Boeing's January 2020 estimate; a 20% failure chance would cut continuing value and services by a fifth), and the option to come back after leaving (valued at zero).
  • Overlay, not a replay: rivals do not react to Boeing's valuation, and the CEO's and the Board's rules (the $1B business-case test, the −$1B downside test, the $2B rating buffer and the $1B tie rule) are applied unchanged.
  • Derivative: the sustaining derivative is always bought. In the low case (25% of the bill) it can cost more than the late-life profit it sustains: at 8.1% the low case needs a slightly higher margin than the brief for a 2045 or 2050 launch.
  • Rates: at 8.1% every line of the brief is re-discounted and the debt penalty re-priced at the new present value; the dashboard's debt logic is not re-run.
