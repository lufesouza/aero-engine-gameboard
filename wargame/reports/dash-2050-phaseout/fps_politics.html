Jobs, Washington, brand and China: can they launch the fps when the numbers say no?

Leaving the narrowbody market would put the weight of Washington, of hundreds of thousands of jobs and of Boeing's identity as an engineering company on the decision, and would leave the market to Airbus and, in time, COMAC. This page looks at how Boeing has behaved when that kind of pressure was on it, sizes what can be sized (government money and a political cost of leaving), and asks how much weight the Board would have to put on staying in to launch the fps, against what Boeing has actually paid for such reasons.

Basis. The franchise page's full view (the brief plus continuing value, services, stranded overhead, widebody erosion and closing costs), constant 2026 $B, against Do Nothing, in the columns the CEO expected each round, at the game's 10.5% and at 8.1%; base values unless a case is named. Rivals' moves are held as the game played them. Strategic premium: the weight the Board would put on staying in, added to every plan with an fps in every column, found as the smallest amount at which Boeing's decision rule launches one. Decision-year money: that premium carried forward at the rate to the year of the decision, in constant 2026 dollars: what the Board would be giving up when it decides. What Boeing paid in the past is in 2026 prices at the time it was paid, so it is compared with decision-year money. Rating test: the Board's check that the plan's risk-case column is no more than $2B below Do Nothing. The rule as re-implemented here gives back all 922 decisions stored by the franchise model and matches the game's rule on 12,000 random cases.

The answer

Pressure has changed where and how Boeing builds, not whether Washington, jobs and reputation have moved Boeing on government contracts it could not walk away from, on safety, on where it builds and on what terms, and on keeping the 737 line together through the grounding. They have not made it launch a commercial programme whose numbers failed: it cut jobs, moved the 787 out of Washington state, shelved the NMA, left the 100-150-seat segment and walked away from Embraer. The one it kept through losses, the 747-8, it kept on product grounds ("a good product that serves a good market") and ended in 2023. Boeing has never been tested on leaving its core 737 market, so the record bounds the answer rather than settling it.
At 10.5% the premium needed is 1.6 to 5 times the most Boeing has chosen to pay up front To launch in 2030 the Board would have to value staying in at $5.48B (PV at 2026), $8.2B in 2030 money, after the franchise terms, about 2% government co-funding and a political cost of leaving ($0.12B at 2026). $1.08B of that is the cost of beating the 787 Re-engine the CEO prefers and the Board vetoes; against leaving alone it is $4.40B ($6.6B in 2030 money). Later rounds need less at 2026 but more in their own money: $10.1B in 2035, $20.2B in 2045, $25.6B in 2050. The most Boeing has chosen to pay up front for such reasons is about $5.1B in 2026 prices (keeping the 737 line in 2020, mostly ordinary value), and to stay in a commercial segment about nothing. It has absorbed larger losses after the fact on contracts it could not walk away from (the KC-46, about $11.2B), but never chose them up front. At base values, if the rating test reads cash only, no premium of any size launches the fps in 2030, 2035 or 2045: the balance sheet binds. With the franchise and government terms at their high values, though, the 2030 and 2035 premiums fall to $2.5B and $2.6B in decision-year money, within what Boeing has paid, and the cash-only rating test no longer blocks them: then politics and brand could tip a launch even at 10.5%.
At 8.1% it is within reach, and it is a choice between airplanes, not about leaving In 2030 at 8.1% the premium needed is $0.50B ($0.68B in 2030 money), inside the CEO's $2B allowance for soft choices (a game rule). It rests on the base franchise terms, and on co-funding and the cost of leaving, which supply $0.88B of the $1.38B needed without them; with the franchise terms low it is $7.9B in 2030 money. The fps already beats Do Nothing here; what it has to beat is a 787 Re-engine, which the CEO prefers and the Board then vetoes on its downside test. "Boeing's an airplane company ... we need to develop a new airplane" (Ortberg, 2024) is the kind of weight that could tip it. If the CEO anticipates that veto instead of proposing the Re-engine, Boeing launches the 7-year fps at Renton with no premium and no politics at all: on the franchise terms alone it is +$2.13B against Do Nothing. The franchise page's "Boeing does nothing" at 8.1% is the game's rule as written, this page's "as played".
Exit pressure bites at the point of no return, and there it would buy a late launch at most Not launching in 2030 is not leaving: Boeing can still launch in 2035, 2045 or 2050, and a 10-year fps launched in 2050 enters service as the last MAX is built. The weight of leaving falls on the 2050 decision, where the premium would have to be worth $25.6B in 2050 money at 10.5% ($20.6B at 8.1%). The game's $1B tests are in 2026 money, about $11B in 2050 money; stated in 2050 money instead, the premium would be $24.6B. Or a government would have to carry 78.0% of the bill (52.3% at 8.1%). That fits Boeing's record: jobs and politics have bought delay, not reversal.
A stronger COMAC lowers the commercial case; Brazil is at odds with Washington, and Embraer could become a third maker China is a competitor in US strategy, not a declared enemy (the December 2025 National Security Strategy frames it mainly as an economic, "near-peer" competitor), and the Pentagon listed COMAC as a Chinese military company in January 2025. Brazil is still formally a US Major Non-NATO Ally (since July 2019), but its government and Washington are at loggerheads: a 25% US tariff on most Brazilian goods since July 2026 (civil aircraft exempt), and Lula, re-elected on 25 October 2026, ran against US pressure. Korea's president has asked Brazil to build a next-generation airliner together; the Embraer page prices Embraer as a third maker, on price and share. If COMAC grows to take all of China's single-aisle demand by 2050, the fps is worth less (−$4.99B against Do Nothing in 2030, from −$4.07B) and the premium needed rises (to $6.30B at 10.5% and $2.17B at 8.1%), even with a higher chance that Washington makes Boeing pay for leaving (an assumption).

How Boeing has behaved under pressure

Where pressure changed the decision

  • Air Force One (2018). A fixed-price deal of about $4B accepted under presidential pressure; about $3.1B of losses since. Calhoun: "risks that Boeing probably shouldn't have taken" (BX-0314).
  • Keeping the 737 line (2019-21). Kept building the grounded MAX in 2019, citing "600-some suppliers, hundreds of thousands of jobs" (Muilenburg, B-1584), then paid about $4B to keep the production system through the 2020 suspension (B-1618, B-1675).
  • Safety under Senate pressure (2024). Withdrew the MAX 7 exemption request (B-2140).
  • Terms, through site competitions. Washington's $8.7B for the 777X (2013); South Carolina's 2009 package for the second 787 line.
  • Selling through Washington (2025-26). Trade-deal orders, and a 200-aircraft China commitment in May 2026. Ortberg: "my primary objective was to reopen that market to our narrowbody airplanes" (Transcript Digest (4)).

Where it did not

  • Jobs. About 10,000 cut in 2009 (B-0147), 26,000 in 2020 (B-1767) and about 17,000 (10%) in 2024 (announced 11 October 2024, press reports; aimed at overhead, B-2206).
  • Washington state. The second 787 line went to South Carolina in 2009 (B-0218) and all 787 production in 2020, which Governor Inslee called "an insult".
  • Segments. Production of the 757 (2004), 717 (2006) and 747 (2022) ended, and the 767 freighter ends in 2027; the NMA was shelved in 2020 (B-1620); the 100-150-seat segment was left to the A220 and E2 (B-1666).
  • Embraer (2020). Walked away from the $4.2B joint venture when cash was at risk (B-1674); the 2024 settlement was $150M.
  • Government money with strings. Raised $25B of bonds rather than take CARES Act loans (2020, BX-1636; it took a general $1.2B CARES tax benefit), and $24B of public equity in 2024 (B-2258; no state support is documented as on offer).
  • China. Out of guidance from late 2022 (B-1971): "our $10 billion in '25, '26 ... has no China" (Calhoun, 2023, B-2052).
  • The next airplane. "That day is not today, and it's not any time near" (Malave, March 2026); "nothing has changed" (Ortberg, 27 October 2026).

What Boeing's leaders say about the next airplane

"Boeing's an airplane company. And at the right time in the future, we need to develop a new airplane" (Ortberg, October 2024, B-2217). "We'll do one" (Calhoun, 2020, B-1620). Always on three conditions: "when is the market ready? ... when are we ready, both financially and from a capacity perspective? ... when is the technology ready?" (Ortberg, 2025, B-2303). And earlier: "It will not be based on market share" (Muilenburg, 2016, B-1131).

Washington and jobs

  • The jobs. About 172,000 employees at the end of 2024 (15% outside the US) and about 182,000 at the end of 2025, about 65,000 of them in Washington state; IAM 751 represents more than 30,000 machinists, and 33,000 struck for 53 days in 2024. Boeing says it supports about 1.5 million US jobs and the Commerce Department says 1.8 million: multiplier estimates, not headcount.
  • The leverage. The US government provided 35-51% of Boeing's revenue in 2020-25; Boeing won both sixth-generation fighter awards (F-47 in 2025, F/A-XX in 2026); the FAA sets the 737 rate cap (38, then 42, then 47 a month); the 737 MAX criminal case ended in a non-prosecution agreement and was dismissed in November 2025.
  • Washington as salesman. In 2025 Boeing and GE made up about $215B of the $244B of foreign deals the Commerce Department helped win, and the May 2026 Trump-Xi summit brought a 200-aircraft China commitment.
  • Washington as shareholder. Since 2025 the government has taken equity in strategic firms (Intel 9.9%, MP Materials about 15%, a golden share in US Steel). Asked on CNBC in August 2025 about stakes in firms such as Boeing and Palantir, the Commerce Secretary said stakes in defence contractors were being discussed and named Lockheed Martin; no Boeing stake has been taken. Boeing has avoided state money with equity strings: it raised $25B of bonds rather than take CARES Act loans in 2020 (taking only a general $1.2B CARES tax benefit), and $24B of public equity in 2024.
  • What leaving would leave behind. Airbus already builds A320s and A220s in Mobile, Alabama (three lines, more than 2,000 staff), so not all final-assembly jobs would leave the US. No US official or member of Congress was found asking for a 737 successor in the searches; that may be a gap in the searches.

Brand and engineering identity

  • In the narrowbody, finance has won since 2011 (this page's reading of the record): the MAX was chosen over the clean sheet its engineers wanted (2011), the NMA was shelved (2020), and $43B went to buybacks in 2013-19, about twice R&D. Boeing did launch the clean-sheet 787 (2004) after dropping the Sonic Cruiser for it, and the 747-8 and 777X derivatives. In 2011, when share was at risk, it still took the cheapest option.
  • Boeing's reputation costs came from failing to execute, not from declining to launch. The MAX crisis cost about $20B directly, Boeing's market value fell from about $240B to about $112B, and it needed a $24B equity raise. Buyers came back once supply did: 1,173 net orders in 2025 against Airbus's 889, and Brand Finance put the brand up 34% in 2026, with no new airplane. Only about 3% of passengers rank aircraft type first.
  • Talent cuts both ways. Boeing hired 10,000 engineers in 2022 without a new airliner, and the F-47 and F/A-XX give engineers frontier work. What erodes is design experience: average engineer tenure fell from 16.4 to 12.6 years over the decade to 2024 (FT, from SPEEA data; partly the 2022 hiring), and across the US military aircraft industry design starts per team fell from about 2.5 a decade in the 1950s to about one every two decades by 1990 (RAND, 1992).
  • The brand risk runs both ways. A launch that fails costs more reputation than a launch deferred: the 787's overruns and the MAX show the downside a brand premium would have to be set against.

China and COMAC

  • The weight. China was 21.5% of Commercial Airplanes revenue in 2018 and about 10% of the commercial backlog in April 2025 (B-2292). Boeing's 2026 China outlook has 7,215 single-aisle deliveries in 2026-45, about 21% of world single-aisle demand and about 360 a year.
  • Boeing's behaviour. Since late 2022 it plans without China (B-1971, B-2052). When China stopped deliveries (2019-24, and April-May 2025) it waited and remarketed aircraft (B-2293). Ortberg calls the 2026 China order "100% dependent" on the US-China talks.
  • Washington's behaviour is transactional. Export licences for COMAC were suspended in May 2025, restored in July 2025 and capped in October 2026 as leverage; the May 2026 deal paired Boeing orders with US "strict supply guarantees" for aircraft engine parts (which COMAC needs; the link to COMAC is an inference). A July 2026 Section 232 finding said aircraft imports threaten national security but set no tariffs.
  • COMAC is slow but backed by the state. About 13-15 C919 deliveries in 2025, against a target raised from 30 to 75 and then cut to 25; a target of 200 a year by 2029; no Western approval before about 2028-31; LEAP engines until China's CJ-1000A arrives around 2030; $49-72B of state support (CSIS estimate). Calhoun in 2023: "even in an optimistic case, maybe in 2050, they get to be a 10% player" (BX-0380).
  • In the game COMAC sells 250 a year from 2050 to its home market first, and gains none of the 955 deliveries Boeing gives up when the MAX ends in 2045-60: they go to Airbus (465) and an unnamed entrant (490).

Brazil and Embraer

  • Allied on paper, at odds in practice. Brazil has been a US Major Non-NATO Ally since 31 July 2019. The July 2025 US tariffs on Brazil were over the Bolsonaro prosecution; civil aircraft and parts were exempt from the additional duty, but Brazilian jets still paid the 10% baseline until the Supreme Court struck those tariffs down on 20 February 2026, while EU, UK and Japanese aircraft were back at zero (Embraer "was the only manufacturer to pay tariffs on aircraft exports", March 2026). A 25% Section 301 tariff on most Brazilian goods followed in July 2026 (aircraft exempt), and Lula won re-election on 25 October 2026 running against US pressure (search extracts; see the Embraer page).
  • Tied into the US. Embraer's jets carry more than 40% US content and Pratt & Whitney engines on the E2; it employs about 2,500 people in the US and sells the C-390 to NATO air forces.
  • Small next to the 737, but courted. 78 commercial deliveries in 2025 against about 564 737s a year at rate 47; no narrowbody decision before about 2030; it sounded out Saudi, Turkish, Indian and Korean partners (2024 report, exploratory), and in July 2026 Korea's president proposed building a next-generation airliner together and KAI and Embraer signed a non-binding memorandum.
  • Boeing's own record with Embraer is the clearest case of strategy giving way to cash: $4.2B for 80% of its commercial business agreed in 2018, terminated in April 2020, settled for $150M in 2024.
  • In the game the "fps via Embraer" plan carries a $100B bill on the board, so it never competes, and the game's Embraer-like entrant sells only to customers nobody else can serve. The Embraer page prices Embraer as a competitor and partners for the bill.

Notes

  • The point of no return. The premium is solved round by round, but only the 2050 decision is an exit. The 2030-45 figures value Do Nothing as never launching, as the brief does (the cost of leaving and closing costs are charged to it): they are the premium needed to launch now rather than never, and counting a later launch in Do Nothing would raise them.
  • The Board's dollar tests ($1B business case, -$1B downside, -$2B rating) are in 2026 money in the game. Stated in the decision year's money instead, the 2030 premium at 10.5% would not change ($5.48B at 2026: there the CEO's $1B margin over the Re-engine binds, not a Board test), and the 2050 one would be $2.24B at 2026 ($24.6B in 2050 money).
  • Readings. "As played" is the game's rule with its four readings, as on the main report; the rating-on-cash and anticipating readings are this page's, not the game's.
  • Sizes. Government co-funding and the political cost of leaving are inferences from precedents, labelled so in the research file. The political cost is small ($0.12B at 2026 at 10.5%) because the chance and amount are small (20% x $0.44B a year, about $0.8B in 2045 money); standing in for the 2050 premium would take about $2.9B a year at risk, more than all of the assumed defence profit.
  • Framing checked against sources: Brazil's Major Non-NATO Ally designation (31 July 2019, Federal Register); the China wording of the December 2025 National Security Strategy (Brookings, CFR); COMAC's listing as a Chinese military company (January 2025, Aviation Week); the Supreme Court's IEEPA ruling (20 February 2026, Thompson Coburn).
