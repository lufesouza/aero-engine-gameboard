The fps business case on your conservative market

The decision Boeing faced in 2030: launch a 10-year fps (in service 2040) or Do Nothing, with Airbus launching NGSA the same year. On your market (the conservative demand curve with firm orders inside it, Boeing losing 1 point a year after NGSA but taking Airbus's slack, and Airbus adding 1% capacity a year when short), the fps adds about $157B of operating profit over 2040-2059. But it needs $60.3B first, and its profit arrives late as production ramps up. It is worth −$5.26B against Do Nothing as the game values it, and −$10.84B in cash. Below: the case, why it is so different from the game without supply and demand, and what would have to change for the fps to become Boeing's choice.

The case. A 10-year fps launched in 2030 is worth −$5.26B against Do Nothing at Renton as the game values it (−$5.63B at new sites), and −$10.84B with the bill spent as it goes out. It returns 6.4% against Boeing's 10.5% cost of capital. In Boeing's expected case it is −$4.80B, $5.80B short of the +$1B its Board requires.
Why it paid in the game without supply and demand. There the same decision was worth +$4.30B, and Boeing launched. The dashboard sells every aircraft it forecasts from day one. It books 17,900 fps over 2040-2059, all at fps margins from the first year. Your factories build the fps up slowly: 972 in its first five years and 13,146 over 2040-2059. And Do Nothing is not the disaster the dashboard thinks (−$0.83B against −$3.64B), because Airbus cannot build the share it wins, so Boeing sells every 737 MAX it can build until 2047.
What would make it Boeing's choice. On its own, any one of these: the fps ramping up 2.6 times as fast (90 a month by 2043 instead of 2047); a 34.2% margin instead of 25.6%; a $73M price instead of $55M; or a $44.7B bill instead of $60.3B. More demand or more customer preference cannot do it alone, however large: the slow ramp caps what the fps can add. With the ramp twice as fast, 7% more demand or a 27.0% margin would be enough.

Cash flow

Ten years of development spend, then a slow climb. Production follows your NGSA ramp, so the fps adds little profit until 2043. In 2040 and 2041 it even costs profit: at Renton the 737 MAX lines wind down as the fps ramps up, while with Do Nothing the MAX would have sold all it can build. Cumulative cash bottoms out at −$57.8B in 2041 and turns positive in 2049. Discounted at 10.5%, it never does within the 20 years the game counts.

Why it differs from the game without supply and demand

In the game without supply and demand (dash-2050-obj) Boeing's 2030 brief showed the same 10-year fps at +$2.86B to +$4.30B against Do Nothing, and Boeing launched it. Here the identical decision shows −$5.26B to −$4.39B. Nothing about the fps itself changed: the same bill, price, margin and entry into service. What changed is how many aircraft get built and sold, by whom, and when.

  • The dashboard sells every aircraft it forecasts, from day one. It assumes 2,000 narrowbodies are sold every year and that Boeing builds whatever its share is. The day the fps enters service, every Boeing narrowbody is an fps at the fps margin: 17,900 of them over 2040-2059. In your world the fps is built on a production ramp like Airbus's NGSA plan: 8, 27, 93, 293, 551 aircraft in its first five years, 90 a month only in 2047. Profit that arrives late is worth far less at 10.5%, while the bill is the same. This one change takes $7.61B off the fps. It is the main reason.
  • Do Nothing is not the disaster the dashboard thinks. On the dashboard, once NGSA flies Boeing's share falls 3 points a year to 20%, and Do Nothing is worth −$3.64B. In your world Airbus cannot build the share it wins: its factories make 831-1,385 aircraft a year in 2038-2059. The customers it cannot serve buy 737 MAXs, and Boeing sells every MAX it builds until 2047. Do Nothing is worth −$0.83B. The fps is judged against that better alternative: Airbus's real factories alone move the comparison by −$1.07B.
  • Your demand is smaller when the fps would sell. Your conservative curve averages 1,783 aircraft a year over 2040-2059, against the dashboard's 2,000. With fewer customers there is less for the fps to win: −$1.24B. Putting the firm orders inside demand (option B) pushes new customers later: −$0.71B.
  • Your share rules make Do Nothing better: Boeing loses 1 point a year instead of the dashboard's 3, and takes Airbus's slack. That counts against the fps: −$0.92B averaged over every order of the changes, but only +$0.33B with Airbus's capacity response when added last, because by then Airbus's real factories already cap how much of the market it can take.

What would make the fps Boeing's choice

Boeing decides through its ExCo and Board, and its Board set the tests in 2030. The fps fails three of them, the first by $5.80B. Below: what would have to change, one lever at a time, then in combination. Each is valued exactly as Boeing's 2030 brief valued the decision, in all six cases, with the better site. Nothing else moves.
