Which date is more advantageous for Boeing: 2039. None makes the fps pay on your demand: in Boeing's expected case it is worth −$4.65B to −$4.23B against Do Nothing in every combination, at least $5.23B short of the Board's +$1B. Launched as they stand, and as the brief books the bill, the dates lose about the same: within $0.21B as Boeing's brief counts them, and counted over the same years 2044 loses least by $0.06B to $0.22B. But a date is only worth aiming for if it can become a launch, and there 2039 needs the least: at the same bill, a 32.4% to 32.7% margin against 34.3% to 34.9% for 2041 and 38.3% to 40.0% for 2044, or a bill of $46.9B to $47.4B against $43.5B to $44.5B and $37.1B to $39.0B, in all five timings, under both countings and in every sensitivity. The catch is timing: 2039 at the 10-year bill means launching in 2029, and even the game's 7-year fps must launch by 2032. As the brief books the bill, the later routes need more margin only because their bill is bigger (33.3% to 33.6% launched in 2030, 35.0% to 35.4% in 2032); spent over the development years they need less, because the money goes out later. At the same launch year, the earliest date Boeing can reach needs about the same or less: for a launch in 2031 or 2032, 2039 and 2041 are within about half a point as the brief books the bill (2039 slightly less in four of five timings), and 2039 needs clearly less if the bill is spent over the development years; for a launch in 2034, 2041 beats 2044 either way. So: the earliest date Boeing can commit to, which is 2039 for a launch by about 2032; 2044 is the hardest to make work.
Why the later dates need more. The game counts each fps for 20 years from entry into service, discounted at 10.5%. A later fps sells its aircraft later, so each point of margin is worth less at 2026: one more point is worth $0.79B for 2039 and $0.39B for 2044 (aircraft retiring as chapter 1 sets out). The fps's profit against Do Nothing falls from $16.3B to $8.0B, while the bill and its debt penalty fall from $21.7B to $13.2B: about the same amount, which is why the losses look alike as the brief books the bill. A later fps also waits longer behind NGSA (in service 2037) while Boeing's preference erodes, and an fps in 2044 is counted to 2063, into the years when your demand falls back.
The retirement timing matters little to the ranking. Every timing gives about the same demand over 2038-2060 (42,714 to 43,526 aircraft); later retirement moves the peak from 2042 to 2045. That moves the fps by at most $0.42B and the margin needed by 0.3 points for 2039, 0.7 for 2041 and 1.7 for 2044 (1.8 counted over the same years): small for 2039, real for 2044. Which date loses least as launched is not a robust result: it depends on how Do Nothing is counted and on the demand assumed before 2038. Which date needs the least to become a launch does not.
How Boeing could make each date a launch. 2039: any one of a 32.4% to 32.7% margin ($17.8M to $18.0M of profit an aircraft instead of $14.1M; at today's cost per aircraft, a price of about $59M), a bill of $46.9B to $47.4B, or a ramp 2.0 to 2.9 times as fast (90 a month by year 3 to 4 after entry into service instead of year 7). A partner paying a quarter of the bill is enough only if it takes back at most 3.1% to 4.1% of the fps's profit; one paying half can take up to 31%. Customers preferring the fps by 10 more points cut the margin needed to 29.2% to 29.5%. 2041: a 34.3% to 34.9% margin or a $43.5B to $44.5B bill; a 1.5× ramp plus a partner paying a quarter (taking back at most 11% to 12% of the profit) passes. 2044: a 38.3% to 40.0% margin or a $37.1B to $39.0B bill; a 1.5× ramp plus a free quarter of the bill passes only if aircraft retire 2-4 years later, and otherwise still needs 25.9% to 26.0%. A faster ramp alone would have to be near-instant. More customer preference helps every date but makes none a launch on its own; it is the one lever on which 2044 comes closest.
A caveat: the same aircraft at every date. Only the entry into service moves; the aircraft, its price, margin and appeal do not. A later fps could be a better aircraft (management's 'technology has to be ready'). As the brief books the bill, 2039's lead is thin: if two more years made the 2041 fps preferred by 2.0 to 3.3 more points of customer preference, it would need no more margin than a 2039 fps launched in 2030 (4.0 to 5.4 points to match 2039 at the 10-year bill). 2044 would need 9 to 13 points (11 to 16 to match 2039 at the 10-year bill). Spent over the development years, 2039's lead over 2041 widens from 0.8–1.4 to 3.4–4.4 margin points (2041 at 10 years against 2039 launched in 2030). If the later aircraft would be that much better, waiting can pay; if not, earlier is better.

Which date is better for Boeing

Launched as they stand, every date loses about the same, −$4.65B to −$4.23B against Do Nothing in Boeing's expected case. Which loses least is not robust. Boeing's brief counts Do Nothing to 2060 whatever the fps date; on that count 2039 loses least when aircraft retire as chapter 1 sets out or 1 year later (a near-tie at 1 year) and 2044 when they retire 2-4 years later. Counted over the same years as each fps (2026 to its entry into service + 19), or with your page's own demand before 2038, 2044 loses least in every timing. Turning the fps into a launch is another matter: at the same bill, 2039 needs the least margin, price or bill change in every timing and on both counts (and the least ramp change as the brief counts it), 2044 by far the most. Preference is the exception (below). And at the same launch year, the earliest date Boeing can reach needs about the same or less: see the table.

Reading the levers

  • Margin is unit profit. Today $55M at 25.64% is $14.1M an aircraft (the 737 MAX makes $3.84M). A launch at 2039 needs about $18M, through lower cost, more content or a higher price. Holding today's cost per aircraft ($40.9M), that is a price of about $59M; holding the margin at 25.64% instead (cost rising with price), it would take about $70M.
  • The bill is the dashboard's $60.3B for a 10-year fps. A partner's share is counted first as free money. Real partners (an engine maker, a structures partner, a venture) are paid back through prices or a share of the profit: one that takes the same share of the fps's profit as it pays of the bill makes the launch harder, not easier (at 2039 a quarter-for-a-quarter partner raises the margin needed to 32.8% to 33.1%), because Boeing still loses the 737 profit the fps replaces. Spending the same money earlier would cost more in today's money (10.5% a year), not less.
  • Ramp is how fast the fps fills its line. 2039 needs 90 a month by year 3 to 4 after entry into service, against year 7 on the game's ramp. At 2044 only a near-instant ramp is enough.
  • Preference is a strong lever with a ceiling. At today's preference Boeing uses only 64% to 83% of its narrowbody capacity over the fps's 20 years: it is short only while the fps ramps up. More preference fills the rest: 10 more points is worth $2.2B to $2.3B for 2039, $1.9B to $2.0B for 2041 and $1.5B to $1.7B for 2044. It stops helping once Boeing builds everything customers want or its factories are full; at that ceiling the fps reaches −$0.99B to −$0.62B (2039), −$0.52B to −$0.48B (2041) and −$0.22B to −$0.03B (2044), still short of +$1B. Here the order reverses: the 2044 fps has the most room to fill, so it comes closest.
  • Fundable. Boeing's cash test holds in every combination: in the game's cash view net debt is repaid by 2030 and stays repaid even in the stress case.
