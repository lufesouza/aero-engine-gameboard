The war game on your conservative demand case

We replayed the war game (ExCo and Board agents, Control's objectives, rounds 2030, 2035, 2045 and 2050) on your new market assumptions. Demand is your conservative-case curve, and the 12,099 open firm orders are its first slice, not extra (option B). Boeing loses 1 point of preference a year after NGSA enters service, but never falls below the share Airbus cannot build. Airbus adds 1% capacity a year while it is short. Every company played exactly the moves of the previous game: Airbus launched NGSA in 2030, the engine makers settled by 2035, and Boeing never launched the fps. What changed is the money. The fps case is much worse: a 10-year fps at Renton launched in 2030 is worth −$5.26B against Do Nothing, against −$2.19B before. And with Do Nothing, Boeing's deliveries fall from 684 a year in 2047 to 248 by 2060 (16% of the market).

The same moves as the previous game, round for round. Airbus launched NGSA in 2030 on the Alt plan with all three engine makers. Pratt & Whitney's and Rolls-Royce's 'launch if selected' orders were triggered, and CFM ordered the GEnx upgrade. In 2035 Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce formed the Joint Venture, and CFM launched its ducted engine and the Embraer partnership. Everyone held in 2045 and 2050. The same tests stopped Boeing's 787 Re-engine and Airbus's A350 Re-engine.
The fps case got much worse: the conservative curve is the main reason. In 2030 a 10-year fps was worth −$5.26B against Do Nothing at Renton and −$5.63B at new sites, against −$2.19B and −$2.74B in the previous game. On the dashboard alone it is worth +$4.30B. Deliveries take $10.31B off the fps here, against $7.24B before. Your curve is smaller where the fps would sell: 1,772 aircraft a year on average over 2040-2060, against 2,086 in the previous game's market. Stepping from the previous market to this one, the curve alone moves the Renton case by −$2.69B and putting the firm orders inside demand by −$0.71B. Your three share and capacity rules together move it by +$0.33B. The case improved each round, to −$0.85B at Renton in 2050, but all of that rise is discounting, because a later launch's loss is smaller at 2026. Like for like, the case lost $1.09B from 2030 to 2035 and moved by −$0.19B and +$0.22B in the later rounds. It never came near the +$1B that Boeing's Board requires.
With Do Nothing, Boeing sells all it can build until 2047, then fades to 16%. Boeing's preference falls 1 point a year from 40% after NGSA enters service in 2037. But Airbus cannot build all its customers want in most years to 2051, and its slack comes to Boeing. NGSA's transition cuts Airbus's capacity to 831 in 2038, and Boeing's preference rises to 51.7% that year. So the 737 MAX sells all the 684 aircraft it can build every year in 2035-2047, with up to 540 customers waiting for a Boeing slot (2040). Airbus's capacity rises 1% a year from 2036, 13.8% by 2052. From 2048 Airbus can build nearly all its customers want, and Boeing's line runs below capacity: 543 aircraft in 2048 and 248 in 2060. In the status quo Boeing builds 684 a year to 2056 and 619 in 2060. Boeing's ΔPV is only −$0.83B (−$3.64B on the dashboard alone), because the decline comes late and is heavily discounted.
Airbus's capacity response is worth about $3B to Airbus and costs Boeing about $0.5B. Without it Airbus would end at +$11.12B and Boeing at −$0.37B. With 1% a year they end at +$14.09B and −$0.83B, and at 2% a year at +$14.86B and −$0.96B. CFM loses most from it (−$4.43B): it is sole source on the 737 MAX, and the NGSAs that displace MAXs carry it on only a third of their engines. Rolls-Royce gains $1.42B. Airbus ends at +$14.09B, against +$13.25B in the previous game; on the dashboard alone it would be +$45.82B.
The largest money left on the table is still the 787 Re-engine. It was Boeing's best alternative in every round: worth +$3.30B in 2030, +$2.00B in 2035, +$0.74B in 2045 and +$0.45B in 2050. Against Do Nothing it is worth what it was worth in the previous game, because the market rules touch only narrowbodies. In 2030 and 2035 its Board's −$1B downside limit stopped it: it lost more than that if Airbus re-engined the A350 too. In 2045 and 2050 it was below the Board's +$1B business case. Airbus left the A350 Re-engine (+$1.75B in 2030, +$0.57B in 2050) on its CFO's cash and robustness tests, and later on his +$1B test and its operating head's engine readiness test.
The engine makers settled early; CFM and Pratt & Whitney lose more in this market, Rolls-Royce gains. CFM ends at −$28.04B (previous game −$23.47B), Rolls-Royce at +$11.03B (+$9.61B) and Pratt & Whitney at −$1.65B (−$0.48B). With no fps, the 737 stays CFM-only, so Boeing misses its dual-source objective again.

Method and caveats

The game. Run dash-2050-cons: your combined_dash.py dashboard (v3), run by the game master without its interface, with its adjustments, Control's objectives and the supply-and-demand layer, now with your market assumptions (sd_options.json in the run record). There were four sealed rounds (2030, 2035, 2045, 2050), each company deciding through its ExCo and Board agents. Each player saw only its own brief and the public bulletin, and the briefs and rules described the new assumptions. An audit of every file and tool output each agent touched found all four rounds clean. A false alarm in 2045, from the audit script misreading an escaped quote, was fixed and the rounds were re-audited.

Your assumptions, as built. (1) Demand is the conservative curve, times the class share. (2) Option B: each year's demand is served first from open firm orders, split by each maker's remaining orders, and only then from new customers. (3) Boeing's base preference is 40% less 1 point a year from NGSA's entry into service, while no fps is in service. Once an fps enters service the dashboard's share rule applies again. (4) The floor: Boeing's preference is never below the share of new customers Airbus cannot build after its firm orders and waiting customers. It applies in every case, the status quo included. (5) Airbus's capacity rises 1%, compounding from the next year, after each year in which its customers at the base preference want more than it can build; it never falls. Everything else is the previous game's layer: flexible customers (48.7%) take the other maker's free slots, 10% of waiting customers drop out each year, and engine makers sell on the aircraft actually delivered.

Tests. test_sd_options.py checks each assumption on throw-away runs: an empty options file changes nothing, demand follows the curve, firm orders plus new customers add up to demand with no front-loading, the 1-point rule, the floor, and the 1% steps. Runs without the options file are unchanged: the previous game's values are reproduced to the cent (the first row of the step table).

Limits. After 2030 no order raises Boeing's line rate, so a short market shows up as waiting customers. Prices do not respond to scarcity. The dashboard counts each narrowbody programme from 2026 to 19 years after its entry into service, with an unlaunched programme at its default date. Here NGSA and the engine makers' narrowbody value stop at 2056 and Boeing's at 2060, so the later decline is counted only to then.
