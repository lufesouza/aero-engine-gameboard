Narrowbody and widebody war game, 2026-2045: fps three years late

A rerun of the five-player war game with one public assumption changed: Boeing's fps takes 10 years from launch to entry into service instead of 7, so a 2031 launch would enter service in 2041, and each extra year costs 10% of fps capex. Boeing, Airbus, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney and CFM/GE played the same three sealed rounds (to 2030, 2035 and 2045), with the same injects, profiles and leadership teams. Each was an independent agent; only the game master held the full game-theory board.

Players: Boeing, Airbus, Rolls-Royce, Pratt & Whitney, CFM/GE. Rounds: 2026–2030, 2031–2035, 2036–2045. Money: $B; delta PV is each player's full-game present value (to 2026, at its own WACC) versus the status quo in which nobody moves.

Summary

How the fight ended

Boeing never launched fps. With a 10-year development, every fps it tested lost value against doing nothing:

  • round 2: best fps (Joint Venture, GTF2, 10-year ramp-up, with the rate step) -$3.1B, against -$2.4B for Do Nothing and +$1.3B for a 787 Re-engine;
  • round 3: any fps would enter service in 2046 or later, and the best one was still $1.4-2.5B below Do Nothing.

In the first game, fps 2031 entered service in 2038, and launching it with the rate step took Boeing's projection from -$2.3B to +$4.3B.

Boeing turned to the widebody instead. In round 2 it launched a 787 Re-engine on GE's GEnx upgrade (2031, in service 2036) and took the 737 rate step. Airbus then decided not to launch the A350 Re-engine it had signalled for 2037. Boeing's widebody share rises from 58.8% to 68.4% in 2045 and 73.8% in 2050; in the first game it fell to 53.8% and 48.8%.

Airbus took the narrowbody market. NGSA (2028, in service 2035 on the CFM LEAP derivative) ran unopposed through the replacement wave, and Boeing's narrowbody share falls from 42% in 2035 to 32.3% in 2045, 25.0% in 2050 and 20% by 2060. Airbus ends at +$44.5B (first game +$36.5B) and Boeing at +$1.7B (first game +$3.9B).

Two coordination failures again, one of them new:

  • NGSA asked for UltraFan in round 1; Rolls-Royce was waiting for an announcement, so NGSA fell back to the LEAP derivative (as in the first game).
  • Round 3: Rolls-Royce committed the UltraFan widebody in 2036 on Airbus's stated 2037 A350 Re-engine. In the same sealed round Airbus's Board shelved it. The engine has no airframe and, in the final round, cannot be cancelled.

Pratt & Whitney's 2030 GTF2 hedge was cancelled in 2031 with no airframe, as in the first game, so its result is identical (-$2.8B).

Four of the ten assigned objectives were met, as in the first game, but a different four:

  • Airbus: both met (60/40 edge, by 1.6pp in 2040; A320 protected);
  • Pratt & Whitney: credibility;
  • CFM/GE: narrowbody dominance (100% of narrowbody engines from 2035).

Boeing met neither: without fps its narrowbody share falls below the 40% status quo from 2040, so incumbency is lost as well as 50/50. Rolls-Royce met neither, and the open fan never flew: no airframe could enter service on it by 2045.

Key findings

  • Three years of delay removes the fps business case. On Boeing's own go/no-go test the fps failed both the nominal and the slip legs in rounds 2 and 3. Boeing's ExCo chose 'derivatives first' and redeployed into the 787.
  • The delay moves the contest from narrowbody to widebody. Boeing gives up narrowbody share (32% in 2045, 20% by 2060) and gains widebody share (68% in 2045). Its total delta PV falls by only $2.2B, because it saves about $23B of fps capex to 2045.
  • Moving first in the widebody Chicken game paid. Boeing's 2031 787 Re-engine turned Airbus's A350 Re-engine negative (-$0.3B to -$0.5B on standard terms or the GEnx fallback; +$0.1B at best, only on aggressive Rolls-Royce terms), and Airbus's Board dropped it. In the first game Airbus moved first (2035) and Boeing declined the 787 Re-engine.
  • A conditional disclosure was read as a commitment. Airbus's 'subject to Board approval' A350 plan led Rolls-Royce to commit $3.1B of capex one year early under its own 'do not make the aircraft wait' rule. This is the mirror of the first game, where Pratt & Whitney cancelled the engine Boeing then named.
  • CFM/GE won again by not moving. Every airframe ended on its existing engines (LEAP derivative and GEnx upgrade), and it ends at +$19.0B with no new-engine capex. Rolls-Royce did better than in the first game (-$3.7B against -$7.0B) because it launched no narrowbody engine and there was no A350 Re-engine to take its sole-source position.
  • The open fan is out of reach in both games. NGSA committed to the LEAP derivative in 2028, and a late fps cannot enter service before 2046, so no airframe could fly RISE by 2045.

Why the delay matters

To isolate the assumption, the first game's own moves are replayed with only fps's development time changed, from 7 to 10 years. Every other order, inject and market reaction is held fixed, and the value of launching fps is Boeing's delta PV with it minus without it.

What does not change

  • Round 1 was identical. The delay does not touch Airbus's 2028 NGSA decision, and the engine makers' 'no airframe, no engine' rules produce the same fallback to the LEAP derivative.
  • The open fan is out of reach in both games. NGSA committed in 2028, and a late fps cannot enter service before 2046.
  • Engine coordination failures persist under sealed moves. In the first game an engine maker cancelled the engine an airframer named; here an engine maker committed to an airframe that was then withdrawn.

A known delay is not a surprise slip

In this game the delay was public from round 1, so Boeing never launched. Had Boeing launched in 2031 expecting 2038 and then slipped to 2041, the first game's path would end at -7.2 instead of +3.9: worse than never launching (-3.6), because by then the capex is committed.

Round 1 (to 2030): positioning and first commitments (2026–2030)

Supply-chain crunch: running development programmes concurrently costs 50% more from now on. The orders were the same as in the first game:

  • Airbus launched NGSA in 2028 and asked for UltraFan.
  • Rolls-Royce waited for an airframe announcement, and CFM/GE preferred its LEAP derivative, so NGSA flies the LEAP derivative (in service 2035).
  • Boeing did nothing, and published an engine policy: an engine committed by 2034 and ready by 2041.
  • Pratt & Whitney hedged with a next-generation GTF launched in 2030.
  • All three engine makers funded durability upgrades.

The market priced NGSA at x1.03.

Method

How this was played and scored

  • Each player is an independent agent that reads only its own company and executive profiles and its own engine view; a runtime hook enforces this. The game master alone sees the full game-theory board and relays only public moves, statements and chosen disclosures.
  • Orders are sealed and simultaneous each round. Engine makers' orders apply first, so an airframer can pick an engine its maker commits to in the same round.
  • Every number is computed by the war-game engine (wargame/model.py). Volumes are stylised (2,000 narrowbodies and 170 widebodies a year); per-unit values are calibrated from the evidence, and parameters marked PLACEHOLDER in the config are assumptions.
  • The fps delay is a known, public scenario assumption (scenario five-player-2045-fps-delay), not an inject: fps entry into service is launch + 10 years, and each of the three extra years costs 10% of fps capex.
  • Volumes are stylised: 2,000 narrowbodies and 170 widebodies a year, PV to 2026 at each player's own WACC. Per-unit values come from the calibrations.
  • CFM/GE's parameters were reconciled from two independent calibrations. Several remain placeholders: alpha, open-fan capex and value, Embraer partnership, lobbying, strain, derivative capex, and the fps ramp-up multipliers.
  • The assigned objectives come from the Boeing Product Development briefing (Boeing proprietary). The referee scores them, but they never change payoffs.
  • This was one game with one seed, against one first game. The comparison shows how these agents responded to the delay, not a distribution of outcomes.
