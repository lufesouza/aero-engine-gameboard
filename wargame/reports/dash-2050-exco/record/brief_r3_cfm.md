## Public statements (last round)

- **Boeing:** Five years ago we launched a new single-aisle airplane on a ten-year path, with entry into service in 2040. I'll tell you plainly where it stands. The program is on its plan. 2040 is still our date. It's a planning date with headroom in it, not a stretch target. We're in the heavy years now: detailed design, building the test airplanes, and certification ahead of us. That's where programs get into trouble, so that's where we're being most careful. If anything moves, you'll hear it from us early, with the plan to fix it, and we'll reset once.

We're not changing anything else today. We're not starting a second development alongside this one. The 787 is a strong airplane, and it has room to grow from our Charleston capacity. We'll look at its next step once this airplane is in service. Doing less and doing it better is what got us here, and it's what will get this airplane across the line.

A few things haven't changed. We'll certify before we build at rate. We won't take on fixed-price development. We'll keep our supply chain funded and healthy, and we'll keep our engineering teams together through entry into service and the ramp after it. Our engine partner stays CFM. We're funding this from our operations and our debt capacity, with no new equity. Paying down debt stays our priority, and we intend to stay solidly investment grade.

There will be a few years when our customers see a newer airplane flying before ours. We've planned for that. We'll keep earning their business on the 737 MAX the old-fashioned way, by delivering what we promised, when we promised it.

Others will make their plans on their own clock. Ours rests on our own readiness. This is the part of the climb where you keep your hands steady and do the work. The financials will follow our production performance, and we've got a lot of work in front of us.
- **Airbus:** Our core principles of safety, quality, integrity, compliance and security will remain at the heart of all we do.

Our next-generation single-aisle aircraft is progressing on its technology clock. Entry into service remains planned for the second half of the 2030s, once the technologies, the production system and certification have matured, and not before. We are preparing its industrial ramp-up with the same discipline.

We confirm three engine makers on the aircraft: CFM, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce. That gives our customers choice and secures supply.

The A320neo family remains the backbone of our single-aisle business. Its production continues in a disciplined and controlled way, and the people and suppliers who deliver it remain protected.

We continue to strengthen a more sovereign European supply chain. That means securing work packages and critical materials such as titanium and rare earths, and recruiting the engineers who will build the Airbus of the 2030s.

On the widebody side, the A350 family, including the A350F, remains our focus.

We fund this within our financial framework: disciplined investment in our future portfolio, and shareholder returns while preserving financial flexibility.

On trade, we keep working with industry partners on both sides of the Atlantic for a zero-for-zero tariff regime in civil aerospace.
- **CFM/GE:** Safety, quality, delivery and cost, always in that order. That doesn't change this year either. So let me tell you what we know and what we don't.

What we know: two new single-aisle airplanes are launched, and there is a CFM engine on both today. We don't take that for granted. We don't have a birthright on any order, so we earn it the way we always have, by delivering. That starts with the LEAP engines and the spares our customers need right now, on time. Spares don't crowd out new engines.

The new ducted engine that CFM and our partners at Safran launched five years ago stays on its gate: not before 2036. We report against that gate on test, not on slides. If testing tells us it needs more time, the date moves. We don't trade durability for a date. It's the right engine for the airplanes in front of us. It isn't our next-generation engine. That is still RISE. We're all in on that technology, together with Safran, and we're keeping it whole. When that architecture enters service is really not for us to say. The airframers and their airline customers will decide that.

Our work with Embraer is a CFM decision we took with Safran, and it rides on that same engine. Any airframer that wants a CFM engine can have one, and nobody gets locked out. On the widebody, the upgraded GEnx is in service for our 787 customers.

What we don't know: how the next few years play out across the industry. So we plan on the low case, we keep the balance sheet and our capital returns whole, and we're not starting anything new this year. You won't see us pointing fingers; you'll see us solving problems. We get paid in dollars, not in percentages. It's that simple, ain't that hard.
- **Pratt & Whitney:** Let me start with what is not done. Our share of narrowbody engine deliveries has held at 24% since 2026, and that is not where we want to be. GTF2 is in development, not yet proven. We launched it in 2030, when we were named on a new single-aisle, and it is due to be ready in 2036, six years from launch. Time on wing and full-life parts are the standards we have set for it, and we still have to prove them on test before entry into service. We will not compress the programme to make a date. If that date comes under risk, we will say so early, with the plan.

Control what you can control. The GTF fleet and A320neo engine deliveries keep first call on our parts, our shops and our engineers, ahead of GTF2. We have learned our lesson from the prior iterations of the GTF.

GTF2 is offered to any airframer where we can be ready by its entry into service, on standard terms. We will not buy a selection, and there are no launch-style discounts. We're not in the early entry phase, the launch phase.

Today Pratt & Whitney makes a standing commitment to a Joint Venture with Rolls-Royce on a new single-aisle engine. That commitment is ours alone. A Joint Venture forms only if Rolls-Royce also commits. That is Rolls-Royce's decision, and we book nothing until it does. Any Joint Venture would share risk, investment and revenue on aligned, standard terms. Pratt & Whitney keeps its gear and core as its own module, with no technology licence. The Joint Venture would carry GTF2's schedule, not reset it.

We will not speak for any airframer's plans, dates, rates or engine choices. When we've seen it and when we feel good about it, we invest.
- **Rolls-Royce:** So, let me be clear. UltraFan for the narrowbody is in development and on its seven-year clock: ready in 2037, seven years from launch, and not before. The full test programme runs and nothing is compressed. If a phase slips, the engine slips. Support goes in place through partner shops before entry into service. We do not want to underestimate the risks. Partnership has always been our preference, and today we put it on the record: Rolls-Royce commits to a Joint Venture with Pratt & Whitney on aligned terms. That means shared risk and investment, with each side's technology protected. We are actually bringing a technology. If Pratt & Whitney comes to the table, we form it. If not, we deliver on our own. Widebody is our franchise. There is no new widebody engine spend without a Re-engine, and durability work on the Trent fleet goes on. We won't do anything not profitable. I will say that categorically.
