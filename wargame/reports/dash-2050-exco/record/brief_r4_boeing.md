## Public statements (last round)

- **Boeing:** Our new single-aisle airplane has been in service since 2040, on the date we gave you. That's five years of airplanes in customers' hands, and we're climbing the ramp the way we said we would. We take one step at a time, on our KPIs, agreed with the FAA, and only after the line has shown it's stable at the rate it's on. We've come a long way. There's still a lot of work in front of us, and we're not going to hurry the last part of the climb.

We're not launching a new program today. We've looked hard at the 787's next step. It's a strong airplane, and it has room to grow from our Charleston capacity. We'll take that next step when the market, the technology and we are ready. Today all three aren't there. So I'm not going to give you a date for something we haven't launched.

Paying down debt is far and away our priority. We're funding the business from our operations, with no new equity. I'm not going to put a year on the rating before the data supports it. You'll see it in the numbers.

We said we wanted to hold half of the single-aisle market. We're at 43% this year, up from 38% when the airplane came into service, and still climbing. That's short of half, and I won't dress it up. We earn the rest one delivery at a time.

A few things haven't changed. We'll certify before we build at rate. We won't take on fixed-price development. We'll keep our suppliers funded and healthy for the rates in front of us. We'll keep working with CFM, our engine partner, to support those rates.

Others make their plans on their own clock. Ours rests on our own readiness. The financials will follow our production performance.
- **Airbus:** Our core principles of safety, quality, integrity, compliance and security will remain at the heart of all we do.

Our next-generation single-aisle aircraft has been in service since the second half of the 2030s, with engines from CFM, Pratt & Whitney and Rolls-Royce. We continue its production in a disciplined and controlled way, alongside the A320neo family, and we put fleet safety and operational integrity above all. The people and suppliers who deliver both remain protected.

On the widebody side, the A350 family, including the A350F, remains our focus. We keep studying the options for its future on our own technology and industrial clock. We will take decisions when the technology and the case are mature, and not before.

We continue to strengthen a more sovereign European supply chain, with long-term agreements sized to our own programmes. We also continue to recruit the engineers who will build the next generations of Airbus aircraft.

We fund this within our financial framework: disciplined investment in preparing our future portfolio, and shareholder returns while preserving financial flexibility.

On trade, we keep working with industry partners on both sides of the Atlantic for a zero-for-zero tariff regime in civil aerospace.
- **CFM/GE:** Safety, quality, delivery and cost, always in that order. That doesn't change this year either. So let me tell you what we know and what we don't.

What we know: both new single-aisle airplanes are in service, and there's a CFM engine on both. The ducted engine CFM and Safran launched in 2030 has been in service since 2036. Our work with Embraer has been in service since 2037, and the upgraded GEnx on the 787 since 2033. Those are dates on the record, not on slides. We didn't take those positions for granted then, and we don't now. We don't have a birthright on the next order, so we earn it the way we earned the last one: by delivering. That means LEAP and the new engine, and the spares behind them, on time to every customer we serve. Spares don't crowd out new engines.

What we don't know yet is how these new engines hold up over years of service, especially in the hot and harsh places where engines work hardest. So we'll measure time on wing on the fleet, in cycles, against the yardstick our customers know, and we'll report it on field data, not on analysis. Where we find something, we fix it with Safran, the airframers and the regulators. We don't trade durability for delivery.

The ducted engine is the right engine for the airplanes in front of us. It isn't our next-generation engine. That is still RISE. We're all in on that technology with Safran, and we're keeping it whole. When that architecture enters service is really not for us to say. The airframers and their airline customers will decide that.

We're not starting anything new this year. We plan on the low case and keep the balance sheet and our capital returns whole. Any commitment on CFM capacity, we make together with Safran. You won't see us pointing fingers; you'll see us solving problems. We get paid in dollars, not in percentages. It's that simple, ain't that hard.
- **Pratt & Whitney:** Let me start with what is not where we want it. Our share of narrowbody engine deliveries was 23.2% in 2044, down from 24.8% in 2039. That is not where we want to be, and we will not buy it back with price. We earn it with time on wing, and with engines delivered when we promised them.

What is done: our Joint Venture engine with Rolls-Royce, which carries GTF2's design, has been ready since 2036 and in service on NGSA since 2037. The Joint Venture formed in 2035 on the terms we published. It shares risk, investment and revenue. Pratt & Whitney's gear and core remain our own module, with no technology licence, and each partner's technology is protected.

Control what you can control. The fleet is where this engine earns its place, and the fleet record is how we will be judged: time on wing, life-limited-part life and shop visits. We will report what that record shows, not what we hope it shows, and we give no dated recovery. If we find a durability or safety issue, we will say so early, with the plan. The GTF fleet, the new engine fleet and A320neo engine deliveries keep first call on our parts, our shops and our engineers. We have learned our lesson from the prior iterations of the GTF.

The GTF Hot Section durability kit stays on offer at shop visits, on standard terms. There are no launch-style discounts. We're not in the early entry phase, the launch phase.

We are not starting a new engine programme today. Any new engine we commit will follow an airframer's selection. It will be ready six years from launch, with durability proven before entry into service. Our capital priorities have not changed: the dividend first, then debt, then share repurchase.

We will not speak for any airframer's plans, dates, rates or engine choices.
- **Rolls-Royce:** So, our narrowbody engine with Pratt & Whitney is in service, and our job now is the fleet. Safety is at the top. Then time on wing, full-life parts and support through our partner shops. We do not want to underestimate the risks. Widebody is our franchise. Durability work on the Trent fleet goes on, and there is no new widebody engine spend without a Re-engine. UltraFan technology continues as technology. We are not starting a new programme this year. Cash goes first to the balance sheet, then to the dividend, then to distributions from sustainable free cash flow. We are not after market share. We won't do anything not profitable. I will say that categorically.

## Other moves made public

- **Boeing:** fps ramp stays KPI-gated, step by step with the FAA — The fps goes from 860 deliveries in 2045 to 940 in 2049. Each production step is gated on KPIs and agreed with the FAA, with 2-3 months of stability at a rate before the next step. On Pope's arithmetic that is about one step of 5 a month every three years, about 17% of doctrine's maximum tempo. If a quality, FAA or supply-chain problem goes live, the ramp holds at its current rate until the KPIs show stability again [BX-1271, BX-1259, BX-1322].
- **Boeing:** Chief Engineer and Chief Aerospace Safety Officer report directly to the Aerospace Safety Committee each year — The report covers fps in-service safety and reliability, CFM ducted durability in service, supplier and quality KPIs through the ramp to 940, and the 737 and 787 results. The board carries deliveries only, so their results, not delivery counts, are the KPI record. The commitment is public; the reports are internal [BG-0043, BX-1298].
- **Boeing:** Certify before building at rate on any derivative; no fixed-price development — No production-certification concurrency on any derivative, including any future 787 step [BX-1263, B-2228]. Development stays on cost-plus or back-to-back terms [BX-1262, BX-1304]. Neither is priced on the board.
- **Boeing:** 787 grows from Charleston; no Re-engine launch this round — The 787 holds 100 a year on the board. Charleston has headroom above that [BX-1317, B-2336]. We give no Re-engine date. Internally, readiness work on the 787's next step continues without a launch: engine durability data and customer requirements. Pope finds no matured widebody engine for a 2050 EIS [BX-1296].
- **Airbus:** Resize the NGSA and A320 supply agreements down to our own needs — This is Wagner's finding, and I adopt it. The long-term agreements for engines from all three makers, the wing work packages, titanium forgings and rare earths were sized for the ramp to the 2039 peak of 1,260 narrowbody deliveries. Our own needs fall to 1,140 in 2045 and 1,060 from 2049. The ECSC resizes each agreement to those needs this year. Holding slots at the peak while our needs fall would be over-contracting, and we will not do it. Any agreement committing more than EUR 300m comes to the Board. In public we present this as a more sovereign European supply chain sized to our own programmes [AX-0060, AX-0062, AX-0063, A-0331, A-0457].
- **Airbus:** Open European engineering recruitment continues — Ordinary public hiring, sized to NGSA in-service support, the A320neo transition and the staged studies. It never draws on ramp staff and never targets any rival's programme teams. The ECSC reviews it this year [A-0366, A-0485].
- **Airbus:** Zero-for-zero trade advocacy — We continue to press, through the industry coalitions on both sides of the Atlantic, for a tariff-free regime in civil aerospace [AX-0088].
- **CFM/GE:** In-service safety and durability reporting on the Ducted, Embraer, LEAP and GEnx fleets, on field data — We report time on wing in cycles, measured on the fleet against the CFM56 yardstick, or we don't report it [CX-0009, CX-0008].
- Hot and harsh service goes first on the Ducted (in service since 2036 on fps and NGSA) and on Embraer (since 2037). That is where LEAP first fell short (Ali, inference from the LEAP history; not a flag).
- Gate calls are made by one cross-functional supply, engineering and quality team, with Safran's engineers on CFM engines. No single person makes a safety call [CX-0012, CX-0633].
- Any flag reaches the Board before the 2050 orders, together with its fleet action: root cause, inspection, a parts fix turned on and turned off in test, then shop visits and spares. Enhanced inspection of safety-critical parts stays in new-make shops and MRO.
- Durability is never traded for delivery or fuel burn [CX-0208].
- We make no claim that the fleet is durable until field data shows it.
Unpriced.

Spares never crowd out OE engines [CX-0529, CX-0542]. No rate, unit or growth number is published, because my ramp calls ran hot in 2023-24 [CX-0590, CX-0592]. Unpriced; not on the board.
